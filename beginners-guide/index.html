    Your first Northwest vegetable garden

    Everything a first-timer needs, in plain language. You don't need a big yard or any experience: one small bed and five easy crops is a great first year.

    Eight steps to a first garden

    1. Pick the sunniest spot you have

      Most vegetables need at least six hours of direct sun a day; tomatoes, squash and peppers want eight or more. Watch your yard on a sunny day and note where the light lasts longest. Leafy crops like lettuce, kale and chard can get by with a little less.

      Close to the house is better than a perfect spot far away. A bed you walk past every day gets watered, weeded and picked; one at the back of the yard gets forgotten by July. Check that a hose reaches it.

    2. Start small

      A single 4 × 8 foot raised bed, or a few large containers on a patio, is plenty for a first year. Raised beds suit the Northwest well: they drain after heavy rain and warm up faster in spring than the ground does.

      Four feet is about as wide as you can comfortably reach into from both sides without stepping on the soil, which keeps it loose. If you're growing in pots, bigger is easier: small pots dry out fast in summer.

    3. Feed the soil

      Fill the bed with a mix of garden soil and compost, or loosen existing soil and mix in two to three inches of compost. Most Northwest soils run acidic, so a soil test (your county Extension office can help) tells you whether to add lime.

      Don't work soil while it's wet. If a squeezed handful stays in a sticky ball, wait a few dry days; if it crumbles, it's ready. Digging wet clay, common west of the Cascades, leaves hard clods for the rest of the year.

    4. Learn your frost dates

      Your last spring frost date decides when tender crops can go out, and your first fall frost date ends the summer garden. They change a lot across the region:

    5. Grow easy crops first

      Radishes, lettuce, bush beans, zucchini and kale are forgiving and quick. Leave peppers and big pumpkins for year two.

      Buy young plants (starts) for tomatoes and anything slow, and sow seeds directly for quick crops like radishes, beans and lettuce. That way your first harvest comes within a few weeks while the slower plants catch up.

    6. Plant on your local dates

      The biggest beginner mistake here is planting warm-season crops too early. A sunny April week doesn't mean the soil is warm, and tomatoes or beans planted into cold soil sit and sulk or rot. Enter your ZIP on the home page to get dates for where you live.

      Cool-season crops like peas, lettuce, spinach and kale are the opposite: they go in early, while it's still chilly, and struggle once summer heat arrives. Getting those two groups in the right order is most of what a planting calendar does.

    7. Water deeply, not daily

      Once summer dries out, give the garden about an inch of water a week (more east of the Cascades) in one or two deep soakings. A soaker hose or drip line on a timer makes this nearly automatic, and keeping leaves dry helps prevent disease.

      A layer of straw, shredded leaves or compost on the soil keeps it moist longer and holds down weeds. Seedlings and new transplants are the exception to "not daily": keep them damp until their roots settle in.

    8. Stay ahead of slugs, and keep notes

      Slugs are the Northwest gardener's number one pest, especially for seedlings and lettuce. Check after rain and at dusk, and use iron phosphate bait, which is safe around pets and food crops.

      Jot down what you planted and when, and what did well. By your second spring you'll know your own garden better than any chart. Stuck on a word? The glossary explains terms like hardening off, bolting and row cover.

    New to gardening? Start with these 5 easy crops

    They're forgiving, quick and hard to kill. Tap one for planting dates and step-by-step instructions.

    • Radishes Ready in about four weeks, so you see results fast.
    • Lettuce Grows in cool weather and a bit of shade, and you can pick it for weeks.
    • Bush beans Big seeds that are easy to plant, and one sowing feeds you all summer.
    • Zucchini and summer squash Two plants are plenty. It almost always produces.
    • Kale Shrugs off Northwest cold and keeps going into winter.

    First-year mistakes to skip

    Planting tomatoes in April

    Warm-weather crops planted into cold soil stall or rot. Wait for your town's date, even if a nursery is already selling them.

    Planting everything at once

    One big planting day means one big harvest and then nothing. Sow lettuce, radishes and beans in small batches a few weeks apart instead.

    Too big, too soon

    A large garden in year one usually ends up weedy by August. One well-kept bed teaches more and gives more food than three neglected ones.

    Common questions

    What is the easiest vegetable to grow in the Northwest?

    Radishes, lettuce, kale, bush beans and zucchini are the most forgiving. Radishes are ready in about a month, and kale keeps producing from spring into winter on the west side.

    When should a beginner start a garden in Oregon or Washington?

    Start in early spring. Build or fill the bed, then plant peas, lettuce and other cool crops as soon as the soil is dry enough to crumble in your hand. Wait for your town's last frost date, and usually a few weeks past it, before tomatoes, beans and squash.

    Do I need a raised bed?

    No, but it helps in the Northwest. Raised beds drain after heavy rain and warm up sooner in spring. In-ground beds work well in loose, well-drained soil.
