    Gardening terms, explained

    73 words you'll see on seed packets, planting charts and in our guides, in plain language.

    Show
    ABCDFGHILMNOPRSTW

    A

    Amendment
    Anything mixed into soil to improve it, such as compost, lime or sand. Fertilizer feeds plants; amendments change the soil itself.
    Soil and water
    Aphids
    Tiny soft insects that cluster on new growth and suck sap. A hard spray of water and the ladybugs and lacewings that eat them usually keep them in check.
    Pests and problems

    B

    Blossom end rot
    A dark, leathery patch on the bottom of tomatoes or squash. It's caused by uneven watering stopping calcium from reaching the fruit, not usually by too little calcium in the soil.
    Pests and problems
    Bolting
    When a leafy crop like spinach or lettuce suddenly sends up a flower stalk, usually in heat or long days. The leaves turn bitter, so harvest before it happens.
    Plant care

    C

    Chill hours
    Hours of winter cold, roughly 32 to 45°F, that fruit trees, blueberries and some bulbs need before they flower well in spring. Read more
    Frost and weather
    Clay soil
    Heavy soil made of very fine particles. It holds nutrients well but drains slowly and stays cold and wet in spring, common in the Willamette Valley. Raised beds and compost help. Read more
    Soil and water
    Climate normals
    Thirty-year averages of weather published by NOAA. Rainline Garden's frost dates are the 1991–2020 normals for a weather station near each town. Read more
    Frost and weather
    Clubroot
    A soil disease of the cabbage family that swells and distorts roots. It lasts years in the soil and is worse in acidic, wet ground.
    Pests and problems
    Companion planting
    Growing plants together because one helps the other, for example flowers that draw pollinators to squash. Some claims are well tested; many aren't.
    Seeds and planting
    Compost
    Broken-down plant material and manure. Mixed into soil it adds organic matter, feeds soil life and helps both clay and sandy soils.
    Soil and water
    Cool-season crop
    A crop that grows best in cool weather and handles light frost, like peas, lettuce, kale and carrots. Plant these in early spring and again for fall.
    Seeds and planting
    Cover crop
    A crop like crimson clover or cereal rye sown in empty beds over winter to protect and feed the soil. Read more
    Soil and water
    Crop rotation
    Not growing the same family of crops in the same bed two years running. It helps keep soil diseases and pests from building up.
    Seeds and planting
    Curing
    Drying a harvest so it stores well: winter squash in a warm room, onions and garlic in a shady, airy spot.
    Harvest and storage
    Cut-and-come-again
    Harvesting the outer leaves of lettuce, kale or chard and leaving the center to keep growing, for weeks of harvests from one planting.
    Harvest and storage

    D

    Damping off
    A fungal disease that makes seedlings collapse at the soil line. Clean trays, fresh seed-starting mix, airflow and not overwatering prevent it. Read more
    Pests and problems
    Days to harvest
    Roughly how long from planting outdoors until the first harvest. Seed packets list it; it's longer in cool summers.
    Harvest and storage
    Deadheading
    Removing spent flowers so the plant keeps blooming instead of setting seed.
    Plant care
    Determinate and indeterminate
    Determinate tomatoes grow to a set size and ripen their fruit over a few weeks. Indeterminate types keep growing and fruiting until frost and need tall support. Read more
    Seeds and planting
    Drip irrigation
    Tubing that drips water slowly at the base of plants. It uses less water than a sprinkler and keeps leaves dry, which cuts down on disease. Read more
    Soil and water

    F

    First frost date
    The date the first fall freeze usually arrives. Warm-season crops die at the first frost; cool-season crops keep going. Read more
    Frost and weather
    Frost pocket
    A low spot where cold air settles on still, clear nights. Gardens in hollows or at the bottom of a slope can frost a week or more later in spring than the town's frost date. Read more
    Frost and weather
    Frost-free season
    The number of days between the last spring frost and the first fall frost. It's your growing season for tomatoes, squash and other tender crops.
    Frost and weather

    G

    Germination
    When a seed sprouts. Each crop has a soil temperature range where it germinates well; below it, seeds sit and may rot. Read more
    Seeds and planting
    Growing degree days
    A running total of how much warmth a season has had, used to predict when crops ripen and pests hatch. Cool Northwest summers add up slowly.
    Frost and weather

    H

    Hard frost (killing freeze)
    A night cold enough, around 28°F or below for several hours, to kill most tender plants and damage hardy ones. A light frost near 32°F only nips the leaves of tender crops.
    Frost and weather
    Hardening off
    Getting indoor seedlings used to sun, wind and cool nights by setting them outside for a few hours a day, longer each day, for about a week before planting. Read more
    Seeds and planting
    Hardiness zone
    A USDA map zone based on the coldest winter night in an average year. It tells you which perennials survive winter, not when to plant vegetables.
    Frost and weather
    Hardneck and softneck garlic
    Hardneck garlic has a stiff center stalk, bigger cloves and better cold tolerance. Softneck stores longer and can be braided. Read more
    Seeds and planting
    Hilling
    Piling soil or mulch around the base of potato or leek plants as they grow. It keeps potatoes from turning green and blanches leeks. Read more
    Plant care
    Hybrid (F1)
    A variety bred from two parent lines for traits like disease resistance or earliness. Seed saved from a hybrid won't grow true.
    Seeds and planting

    I

    Integrated pest management (IPM)
    Dealing with pests by watching first, preventing problems and using the gentlest fix that works before reaching for sprays.
    Pests and problems
    Interplanting
    Growing a quick crop between slow ones, like radishes between young tomatoes, so the space is used twice.
    Seeds and planting

    L

    Last frost date
    The date after which freezing nights usually stop in spring. Rainline Garden uses the typical (median) date: in half of years the last frost comes later, so tender crops wait longer. Read more
    Frost and weather
    Late blight
    A fast-spreading disease of tomatoes and potatoes that turns leaves and fruit brown and greasy in cool, wet weather. Keeping leaves dry and growing under cover help. Read more
    Pests and problems
    Leggy seedlings
    Seedlings that grow tall, thin and floppy because they don't get enough light. Keep grow lights a few inches above the plants. Read more
    Seeds and planting
    Lime
    Ground limestone added to raise soil pH in acidic soil. Add it only if a soil test says so, and never around blueberries. Read more
    Soil and water
    Loam
    Soil with a good balance of sand, silt and clay. It holds water and nutrients but still drains. Most garden soils can be improved toward it with compost.
    Soil and water

    M

    Microclimate
    The small-scale climate of one spot. A bed against a south-facing wall stays warmer than an open lawn a few yards away.
    Frost and weather
    Mulch
    A layer of straw, leaves, compost or plastic on top of the soil. It holds moisture, smothers weeds and, if dark plastic, warms the soil.
    Soil and water

    N

    N-P-K
    The three numbers on a fertilizer bag: nitrogen for leaves, phosphorus for roots and flowers, potassium for overall health, as percentages by weight.
    Soil and water

    O

    Onion sets
    Small dormant onion bulbs you plant instead of seeds. They're the easiest way to grow onions. Read more
    Seeds and planting
    Open-pollinated and heirloom
    Open-pollinated varieties grow true from saved seed. Heirlooms are open-pollinated varieties that have been passed down for decades.
    Seeds and planting
    Organic matter
    The part of soil made from once-living things. More of it means soil that crumbles, holds water and feeds plants slowly.
    Soil and water
    Overwintering
    Keeping a crop alive through winter for a spring harvest, like garlic, overwintering onions or kale under row cover. Read more
    Harvest and storage

    P

    Perennial
    A plant that lives for years, like asparagus, rhubarb, berries and most herbs. Annuals like lettuce and beans grow, set seed and die in one season.
    Seeds and planting
    Pinching
    Snipping off a stem tip so the plant branches and gets bushier. Basil, dahlias and many flowers respond well to it.
    Plant care
    Pollination
    Moving pollen so a flower can set fruit. Squash, cucumbers and berries depend on bees; tomatoes and beans mostly pollinate themselves. Read more
    Plant care
    Potting up
    Moving a seedling into a bigger pot before it goes in the garden, so its roots have room while it waits for warm weather.
    Seeds and planting
    Powdery mildew
    A white, dusty coating on leaves of squash, peas and others, worst in late summer. Resistant varieties and good airflow help.
    Pests and problems
    Pre-sprouting (chitting)
    Letting seed potatoes or pea seeds start to sprout before planting so they come up faster in cool soil. Read more
    Seeds and planting

    R

    Rain shadow
    The drier area on the downwind side of mountains. East of the Cascades gets a fraction of the rain that falls west of them, so gardens there need far more watering. Read more
    Frost and weather
    Raised bed
    A garden bed built up above the ground, often in a wooden frame. It drains and warms faster in spring, which matters in wet Northwest soil. Read more
    Soil and water
    Row cover
    A light fabric laid over plants. It traps warmth, guards against light frost and keeps insects off. Read more
    Plant care

    S

    Scapes
    The curly flower stalks hardneck garlic sends up in early summer. Cut them off so the bulb grows bigger, and cook them like green onions. Read more
    Harvest and storage
    Seed potatoes
    Potatoes grown to be planted, certified free of disease. Grocery-store potatoes are often treated to stop sprouting. Read more
    Seeds and planting
    Side-dressing
    Adding fertilizer or compost along a row or around plants partway through the season, for hungry crops like corn, squash and brassicas.
    Soil and water
    Slugs
    The Northwest's top garden pest, most active on wet nights in spring and fall. They chew seedlings to the ground; hand-picking, traps and iron phosphate bait work. Read more
    Pests and problems
    Soaker hose
    A porous hose that seeps water along its length. A cheap way to water a bed slowly at the roots. Read more
    Soil and water
    Soil pH
    How acidic or alkaline soil is, from 0 to 14 with 7 neutral. Most vegetables like about 6.0 to 7.0; blueberries want 4.5 to 5.5. Rainy west-side soils tend to be acidic. Read more
    Soil and water
    Soil temperature
    How warm the soil is a few inches down. In the Northwest it lags weeks behind the air, which is why warm crops wait. A soil thermometer costs about $10. Read more
    Soil and water
    Soil test
    A lab or kit check of your soil's pH and nutrients. It tells you what to add, so you don't add lime or fertilizer you don't need. Read more
    Soil and water
    Sow seeds (direct sow)
    Planting seeds straight into the garden soil where they'll grow, instead of starting them in pots.
    Seeds and planting
    Spacing
    How far apart to set plants or seeds. Crowded plants compete for light and water and stay damp, which invites disease.
    Seeds and planting
    Staking and trellising
    Giving climbing or heavy plants something to grow on, like stakes, cages or netting. It keeps fruit off the wet ground and saves space.
    Plant care
    Start seeds indoors
    Sowing seeds in trays inside, under lights or in a sunny window, weeks before it's warm enough outside. It gives slow crops like tomatoes and peppers a head start. Read more
    Seeds and planting
    Starts (transplants)
    Young plants grown in small pots, either by you indoors or bought at a nursery, then planted in the garden.
    Seeds and planting
    Succession planting
    Sowing a short row every two or three weeks instead of all at once, so you get a steady harvest. Read more
    Seeds and planting
    Suckers
    Side shoots that grow where a tomato leaf meets the main stem. Removing some on indeterminate tomatoes ripens fruit sooner in short Northwest summers. Read more
    Plant care

    T

    Thinning
    Pulling out extra seedlings so the rest have room to grow. Carrots, beets and radishes need it.
    Seeds and planting
    True leaves
    The second set of leaves a seedling grows, shaped like the adult plant's. Seedlings are usually ready to thin or move to bigger pots once they have them. Read more
    Seeds and planting

    W

    Wall-o-water and cloches
    Covers that sit over a single plant to keep it warm. A wall-o-water is a ring of water-filled tubes that soaks up heat by day and releases it at night. Read more
    Plant care
    Warm-season crop
    A crop that's killed by frost and needs warm soil, like tomatoes, beans, squash and basil. Plant these after the last frost once the soil warms.
    Seeds and planting
