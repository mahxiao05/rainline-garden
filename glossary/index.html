    Gardening terms, explained

    The words you'll see on seed packets and planting charts, in plain language.

    Last frost date
    The date after which freezing nights usually stop in spring. Rainline Garden uses the typical (median) date: in half of years the last frost comes later, so tender crops wait longer.
    Frost and hard freeze
    A frost here means the air reaches 32°F, which damages tender crops like tomatoes, basil and squash. A hard freeze, about 28°F or colder for several hours, is what finally stops hardy crops like kale and cabbage.
    First frost date
    The date the first fall freeze usually arrives. Warm-season crops die at the first frost; cool-season crops keep going.
    Frost-free season
    The number of days between the last spring frost and the first fall frost. It's your growing season for tomatoes, squash and other tender crops.
    Sow seeds (direct sow)
    Planting seeds straight into the garden soil where they'll grow, instead of starting them in pots.
    Starts (transplants)
    Young plants grown in small pots, either by you indoors or bought at a nursery, then planted in the garden.
    Start seeds indoors
    Sowing seeds in trays inside, under lights or in a sunny window, weeks before it's warm enough outside. It gives slow crops like tomatoes and peppers a head start.
    Hardening off
    Getting indoor seedlings used to sun, wind and cool nights by setting them outside for a few hours a day, longer each day, for about a week before planting.
    Cool-season crop
    A crop that grows best in cool weather and handles light frost, like peas, lettuce, kale and carrots. Plant these in early spring and again for fall.
    Warm-season crop
    A crop that's killed by frost and needs warm soil, like tomatoes, beans, squash and basil. Plant these after the last frost once the soil warms.
    Soil temperature
    How warm the soil is a few inches down. In the Northwest it lags weeks behind the air, which is why warm crops wait. A soil thermometer costs about $10.
    Bolting
    When a leafy crop like spinach or lettuce suddenly sends up a flower stalk, usually in heat or long days. The leaves turn bitter, so harvest before it happens.
    Thinning
    Pulling out extra seedlings so the rest have room to grow. Carrots, beets and radishes need it.
    Succession planting
    Sowing a short row every two or three weeks instead of all at once, so you get a steady harvest.
    Row cover
    A light fabric laid over plants. It traps warmth, guards against light frost and keeps insects off.
    Wall-o-water and cloches
    Covers that sit over a single plant to keep it warm. A wall-o-water is a ring of water-filled tubes that soaks up heat by day and releases it at night.
    Mulch
    A layer of straw, leaves, compost or plastic on top of the soil. It holds moisture, smothers weeds and, if dark plastic, warms the soil.
    Days to harvest
    Roughly how long from planting outdoors until the first harvest. Seed packets list it; it's longer in cool summers.
    Onion sets
    Small dormant onion bulbs you plant instead of seeds. They're the easiest way to grow onions, though young plants (starts) usually make bigger bulbs.
    Seed potatoes
    Potatoes grown to be planted, certified free of disease. Grocery-store potatoes are often treated to stop sprouting.
    Curing
    Drying a harvest so it stores well: winter squash in a warm room, onions and garlic in a shady, airy spot.
    Cover crop
    A crop like crimson clover or cereal rye sown in empty beds over winter to protect and feed the soil.
    Hardneck and softneck garlic
    Hardneck garlic has a stiff center stalk, bigger cloves and better cold tolerance. Softneck stores longer and can be braided.
    Rain shadow
    The dry area on the downwind side of a mountain range. Storms drop their rain on the west slopes of the Cascades, so the east side gets far less.
    Microclimate
    A spot that's warmer, colder, wetter or drier than the town around it, like the bed against a south-facing wall or a low, shady corner.
    Frost pocket
    A low spot where cold air settles on still nights, so frost hits there first in fall and lingers longest in spring.
    Hardiness zone
    A USDA number based on how cold your winters get. It tells you which perennials, shrubs and trees will survive, not when to plant vegetables.
    Compost
    Rotted plant scraps, leaves and manure. Mixed into soil, it feeds plants, loosens clay and helps sandy soil hold water.
    Soil pH
    How acidic or alkaline the soil is, on a scale where 7 is neutral. Most vegetables like it slightly acidic (about 6 to 7); blueberries like it much more acidic.
    Lime
    Ground limestone added to make acidic soil less acidic. Rainy west-side soils often need it; a soil test tells you how much.
    Raised bed
    A garden bed built up above the ground, often inside a wood frame. It drains and warms faster in wet Northwest springs.
    Heirloom
    An old variety passed down for generations. If you save its seeds, you get the same plant next year.
    Hybrid (F1)
    A variety made by crossing two different parents. Hybrids are often vigorous or disease resistant, but seeds saved from them won't grow true.
    Open-pollinated
    Seeds made by natural pollination that grow into plants like the parent. All heirlooms are open-pollinated.
    Determinate and indeterminate
    Determinate (bush) tomatoes grow to a set size and ripen most of their fruit at once. Indeterminate (vining) tomatoes keep growing and fruiting until frost and need tall stakes.
    Pollination
    Moving pollen between flowers so they can turn into fruit. Bees do most of it; squash, berries and fruit trees all depend on it.
    Fruit set
    When a pollinated flower starts to swell into a fruit. Cold nights below about 55°F can stop tomatoes and peppers setting fruit.
    Side-dressing
    Adding fertilizer or compost along a row or around plants partway through the season, when hungry crops start to need more.
    Cordon
    A plant trained to a single main stem, like a tall tomato tied to one stake or string with its side shoots pinched off. Apples and pears can be grown as cordons against a fence too.
    Pinching
    Nipping off the tip of a shoot to make a plant bushier, as with basil, or removing the side shoots (suckers) on vining tomatoes.
    Staking and trellising
    Supports that hold plants up off the ground: stakes or cages for tomatoes, a trellis or netting for peas, pole beans and cucumbers.
    Overwintering
    Keeping a crop in the ground through winter, like garlic, kale, leeks or fava beans, for an early harvest the next year.
    Bare-root
    Plants sold while dormant with no soil around the roots, like fruit trees, berries and asparagus in late winter. They're cheaper and settle in fast.
    Dormant
    Resting for winter, with no leaves or new growth. It's the best time to plant or prune many fruit trees and shrubs.
    Crown
    The spot where a plant's stems meet its roots. Planting the crown too deep can rot strawberries, rhubarb and asparagus.
    Cane
    A long, woody stem on raspberries and blackberries. Most varieties fruit on canes in their second year, then those canes are cut out.
    Runner
    A stem a strawberry plant sends along the ground that roots into a new plant. Pinch them off for bigger berries, or let them fill a bed.
    Garlic scape
    The curly flower stalk on hardneck garlic. Cut it off in early summer so the bulb grows bigger; it's good to eat.
    Late blight
    A disease that spreads fast in cool, wet weather, usually once fall rains start. Leaves get dark, greasy blotches and tomatoes turn brown and firm. Keep leaves dry and pick early.
    Clubroot
    A soil disease that swells and twists the roots of cabbage-family crops. Raising soil pH with lime and rotating crops help keep it away.
    Damping off
    When seedlings suddenly flop over at the soil line, caused by fungi in cold, wet soil. Use clean pots, fresh seed-starting mix and good airflow.
    Blossom end rot
    A dark, sunken patch on the bottom of tomatoes and peppers. It's linked to uneven watering, so water deeply and regularly and mulch.
    Drip irrigation and soaker hose
    Hoses that leak water slowly right at the roots. They save water in dry summers and keep leaves dry, which helps prevent disease.
    Drip line
    The edge of a tree's canopy, where rain drips off the outer leaves. Most of a tree's feeding roots spread out to here and beyond.
    Cold frame
    A bottomless box with a clear lid that works like a small greenhouse, for early spring starts and late fall greens.
    Cut-and-come-again
    Picking the outer leaves and leaving the center to keep growing, so one planting of lettuce, chard or kale feeds you for weeks.
    Inoculant
    A powder of helpful bacteria you coat pea and bean seeds with. The bacteria help the plants pull nitrogen from the air.
    Grafting and rootstock
    Most fruit trees are two plants joined together: the top (the variety) gives the fruit, and the rootstock below sets how big the tree grows.
