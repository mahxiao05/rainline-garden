Gardening terms, explained

The words you'll see on seed packets and planting charts, in plain language.

Last frost date
The date after which freezing nights usually stop in spring. Rainline Garden uses the typical (median) date: in half of years the last frost comes later, so tender crops wait longer.
First frost date
The date the first fall freeze usually arrives. Warm-season crops die at the first frost; cool-season crops keep going.
Frost-free season
The number of days between the last spring frost and the first fall frost. It's your growing season for tomatoes, squash and other tender crops.
Sow seeds (direct sow)
Planting seeds straight into the garden soil where they'll grow, instead of starting them in pots.
Starts (transplants)
Young plants grown in small pots, either by you indoors or bought at a nursery, then planted in the garden.
Start seeds indoors
Sowing seeds in trays inside, under lights or in a sunny window, weeks before it's warm enough outside. It gives slow crops like tomatoes and peppers a head start.
Hardening off
Getting indoor seedlings used to sun, wind and cool nights by setting them outside for a few hours a day, longer each day, for about a week before planting.
Cool-season crop
A crop that grows best in cool weather and handles light frost, like peas, lettuce, kale and carrots. Plant these in early spring and again for fall.
Warm-season crop
A crop that's killed by frost and needs warm soil, like tomatoes, beans, squash and basil. Plant these after the last frost once the soil warms.
Soil temperature
How warm the soil is a few inches down. In the Northwest it lags weeks behind the air, which is why warm crops wait. A soil thermometer costs about $10.
Bolting
When a leafy crop like spinach or lettuce suddenly sends up a flower stalk, usually in heat or long days. The leaves turn bitter, so harvest before it happens.
Thinning
Pulling out extra seedlings so the rest have room to grow. Carrots, beets and radishes need it.
Succession planting
Sowing a short row every two or three weeks instead of all at once, so you get a steady harvest.
Row cover
A light fabric laid over plants. It traps warmth, guards against light frost and keeps insects off.
Wall-o-water and cloches
Covers that sit over a single plant to keep it warm. A wall-o-water is a ring of water-filled tubes that soaks up heat by day and releases it at night.
Mulch
A layer of straw, leaves, compost or plastic on top of the soil. It holds moisture, smothers weeds and, if dark plastic, warms the soil.
Days to harvest
Roughly how long from planting outdoors until the first harvest. Seed packets list it; it's longer in cool summers.
Onion sets
Small dormant onion bulbs you plant instead of seeds. They're the easiest way to grow onions.
Seed potatoes
Potatoes grown to be planted, certified free of disease. Grocery-store potatoes are often treated to stop sprouting.
Curing
Drying a harvest so it stores well: winter squash in a warm room, onions and garlic in a shady, airy spot.
Cover crop
A crop like crimson clover or cereal rye sown in empty beds over winter to protect and feed the soil.
Hardneck and softneck garlic
Hardneck garlic has a stiff center stalk, bigger cloves and better cold tolerance. Softneck stores longer and can be braided.
