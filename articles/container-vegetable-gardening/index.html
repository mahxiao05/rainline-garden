    Growing Vegetables in Containers on a Northwest Balcony or Patio

    A balcony is enough for herbs, greens and cherry tomatoes. Pot sizes, potting mix and the summer watering that pots need.

    By Jigmey Dorjee
    Posted October 2026 · Updated November 2026

    The short answer

    Sun
    Six to eight hours for tomatoes, peppers and cucumbers; four to six for greens and herbs.
    Pots
    At least 5 gallons per tomato, with drainage holes and bagged potting mix, not garden soil.
    Water
    Often. In a heat wave a big tomato in a pot may need water every day.

    You don't need a yard to grow food. If you live in an apartment in Seattle, a townhouse in Portland or a rental where you can't dig up the lawn, a sunny balcony, a patio or a strip of driveway can still produce a surprising amount of salad greens, herbs and tomatoes. There's something about stepping outside your door and cutting lettuce for dinner that never gets old.

    Containers even have some advantages in the Northwest: they drain well in a wet spring, dark pots warm up faster than garden soil, and you can move them to follow the sun or dodge a cold night.

    Start with sun

    Sun matters more than anything else. Fruiting crops like tomatoes, peppers and cucumbers need at least six to eight hours of direct sun. Leafy greens and many herbs get by with four to six. A south- or west-facing balcony is ideal; a north-facing one is best for lettuce, parsley and mint.

    Before you buy anything, spend a sunny day noting when sun actually reaches your space. Balconies are tricky: the floor above can shade the back half, railings cast shadows on low pots, and a building across the street can block the afternoon. Put sun-lovers right at the front edge or up on a stand, and keep the shadier spots for greens and herbs. In midsummer, a west-facing balcony with light walls can also get very hot in late afternoon, and that's when lettuce bolts and pots dry out fastest.

    Pot sizes that work

    • Tomatoes: at least 5 gallons per plant, and bigger is better for tall varieties. Choose compact, bush or "patio" types, or cherry tomatoes.
    • Peppers and eggplant: about 3 to 5 gallons each.
    • Zucchini: a large pot of 10 gallons or more for one plant.
    • Lettuce, spinach, arugula and other greens: a wide pot or window box at least 6 to 8 inches deep.
    • Herbs: 1 to 2 gallons each. Give mint its own pot, because it spreads aggressively.
    • Strawberries: hanging baskets, window boxes or strawberry pots, especially day-neutral varieties.

    Every container needs drainage holes. Fabric grow bags are inexpensive, light and drain well.

    A few words on what pots are made of. Terracotta looks lovely but dries out fast, which is fine for rosemary and thyme but hard on tomatoes in August. Plastic and glazed pots hold water longer. Dark pots warm up in spring sun, which helps in May and can cook roots on a hot July afternoon, so shade the sides of dark pots in summer, or group them so they shade each other. If you're on a balcony, also think about weight: a big pot of wet soil is heavy, and some buildings have rules about it. Fabric bags and plastic are much lighter than ceramic.

    Use potting mix, not garden soil

    Garden soil packs down in a pot, drains poorly and can carry weeds and disease. Use a good-quality bagged potting mix. Refresh it each year by mixing in some compost, or replace it if plants struggled.

    Water and feed more often

    Containers dry out much faster than the ground, especially in July and August, and a big tomato in a pot may need water every day during a heat wave. Water until it runs out the bottom, then check again the next day. A saucer under the pot helps, but empty it after rain so roots don't sit in water.

    Because frequent watering washes nutrients out of the pot, feed container vegetables regularly through the season with a balanced fertilizer, following the label.

    What a small space can actually grow

    It helps to be realistic. A balcony won't keep you in potatoes or winter squash, which need lots of room for a small harvest. Where containers shine is with crops you use a little of often and that are expensive or wilt quickly from the store: salad greens, herbs, cherry tomatoes, a few peppers. A couple of pots of basil and parsley will save you buying those little plastic clamshells all summer. One healthy cherry tomato in a big pot can give you a handful of tomatoes a day at the peak of summer.

    Think vertically too. Pole beans, peas and cucumbers climb a trellis or the balcony railing and take very little floor space. Hanging baskets work for strawberries and trailing herbs.

    Through the seasons

    West of the Cascades, a container garden can run most of the year. Start lettuce, spinach and peas in pots in March, when the ground is still soggy. Move to tomatoes, peppers and basil after the soil warms in May. In late summer, sow kale, chard and more salad greens for fall, and they'll often keep going into winter in a sheltered spot against the house. In a hard freeze, push pots together against a wall or bring them into a garage for a few days, because roots in a pot get much colder than roots in the ground.

    Easy crops to start with

    • Leaf lettuce and other salad greens, which you can cut again and again
    • Herbs like basil, parsley, chives, thyme and mint
    • Cherry tomatoes
    • Bush beans
    • Kale and Swiss chard
    • Radishes
