    Succession Planting: Steady Harvests Instead of All at Once

    Small sowings of lettuce, beans and carrots every few weeks, and a fall crop after the spring one, keep a small bed producing longer.

    By Jigmey Dorjee
    Posted October 2026 · Updated November 2026

    The short answer

    Small sowings
    Radishes every one to two weeks; lettuce every two to three weeks.
    Follow-ons
    Peas then beans, garlic then fall greens, summer crops then a cover crop.
    Last sowing
    Count back from the first fall frost: days to maturity plus about two weeks.

    Plant a whole packet of lettuce in one day and you'll have more salad than you can eat for two weeks, then none at all. Succession planting fixes that. Instead of planting everything at once, you sow a little at a time, or follow one crop with another, so the garden keeps producing all season.

    It's the difference between a garden that has a big moment in June and a garden you can pick from every week from April to November. And in the Northwest, with a long, mild growing season west of the Cascades, you have more room to do it than gardeners in most of the country.

    Method 1: Small sowings every few weeks

    For fast crops that are best picked young, sow a short row on a regular schedule:

    • Radishes: every one to two weeks. They're ready in about four weeks.
    • Lettuce and salad greens: every two to three weeks.
    • Cilantro: every three weeks. It goes to seed quickly in warm weather.
    • Bush beans: every two to three weeks while the planting window is open.

    Keep each sowing inside the crop's planting window. In Portland, for example, Rainline Garden's spring window for lettuce runs Mar 1 – Apr 26, with a second fall window of Aug 20 – Sep 3. Bush beans run May 10 – Jun 21, which leaves room for about three sowings. Your own windows are on your planting calendar.

    How much to sow each time? Less than you think. For lettuce, a few plants per person per sowing is usually enough for regular salads. For radishes, a row a foot or two long. The point is a small, steady supply, and if a sowing fails or gets eaten by slugs, the next one is only two weeks behind.

    The easiest way to keep it going is to tie it to something you already do. Every other Saturday, or every time you pick the last of one sowing, sow the next. A repeating reminder on your phone works too. Most people who give up on succession planting just forget the third or fourth sowing in the middle of a busy summer.

    Method 2: Follow one crop with another

    When an early crop finishes, plant something new in the same space:

    • Peas, then beans. Peas finish as summer warms up, which is when the soil is warm enough for beans.
    • Spring lettuce or spinach, then summer squash or cucumbers.
    • Garlic, then fall greens. Garlic comes out in early summer. Where your fall windows allow, that bed can hold fall lettuce, kale or turnips.
    • Summer crops, then cover crops. When tomatoes and squash are done, sow a winter cover crop.

    Method 3: Plant fast crops between slow ones

    Radishes sown in the same row as carrots come up quickly, mark the row, and are harvested before the carrots need the space. Lettuce can grow between young tomato or squash plants and be picked before those plants fill in.

    Working out the last sowing date

    For fall harvests, count back from your first fall frost using the days to maturity on the seed packet, then add about two weeks, because plants grow more slowly as days get shorter and cooler. Rainline Garden's fall windows already do this math for the crops that suit fall planting.

    Summer sowings need extra help

    Sowing in July and August is different from sowing in April. The soil surface dries out within hours in the sun, and lettuce and spinach seed germinate poorly when the soil is hot. Sow in the evening, water the row well, and lay a board, a strip of shade cloth or a piece of burlap over it to keep it cool and moist. Check it every day and remove the cover as soon as you see seedlings. For lettuce, choose varieties described as slow-bolting or heat-tolerant for summer sowings, and save the delicate ones for spring and fall. The same goes for cilantro: look for "slow-bolt" types.

    One 4 by 8 foot bed through a west-side year

    To give you an idea, this is roughly what a single raised bed can do in a typical Portland or Seattle year. Your own dates will vary.

    • March: peas along a trellis on the north side; spinach and radishes in the rest.
    • April and May: lettuce sown every few weeks as the radishes come out.
    • June: spinach finishes and is replaced with bush beans once the soil is warm.
    • July: peas finish; that strip gets kale and broccoli transplants for fall and winter, and a sowing of carrots.
    • August: beans keep going; more lettuce, spinach and radishes as the summer lettuce bolts.
    • October: beans come out and garlic goes in.

    That's eight or nine crops from one small bed, instead of the two or three you'd get planting it once in May.

    Keep the soil fed

    A bed that grows two or three crops a year uses more nutrients. Mix in an inch of compost between crops to keep it productive.
