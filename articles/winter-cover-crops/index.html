    Winter Cover Crops for Northwest Vegetable Beds

    Crimson clover, fava beans or cereal rye sown in early fall hold bare soil through the winter rain and feed it by spring.

    EHBy Elaine Holloway
    Posted October 2026 · Updated November 2026

    The short answer

    What
    Crimson clover, Austrian winter peas, fava beans or cereal rye.
    When
    Late summer to about early October west of the Cascades; earlier on the east side.
    Spring
    Cut when legumes start to flower, then wait two to three weeks before planting.

    West of the Cascades, a bare vegetable bed spends the winter getting pounded by rain. That rain compacts the surface, washes away fine soil and leaches nutrients down past where roots can reach. A cover crop sown in early fall protects that bed all winter, and in spring you turn it into food for the soil.

    What a cover crop does

    • Protects the soil from heavy rain and erosion.
    • Adds organic matter when you turn it under, which improves drainage in clay and water-holding in sandy soil.
    • Holds onto nutrients that would otherwise wash away over winter.
    • Adds nitrogen, if you grow a legume such as clover, peas or favas.
    • Smothers winter weeds.

    Good choices for Northwest gardens

    • Crimson clover. A legume with striking red flowers in spring. Easy for home gardens and fixes nitrogen.
    • Austrian winter peas. A cold-hardy legume, often sown mixed with a grain for support.
    • Fava beans. Large-seeded and easy to sow by hand. Grow them as a cover crop, or let some mature for a spring harvest. See fava bean timing.
    • Cereal rye. Very hardy and great at holding soil and smothering weeds, but it grows tall and tough in spring. Cut it early.

    A mix of a legume and a grain, like peas with cereal rye, combines nitrogen with lots of organic matter.

    When to sow

    Sow as early in fall as you can clear a bed, ideally from late summer through about early October west of the Cascades. Legumes in particular need time to establish before the cold. Late sowings still help, but give less growth. In colder east-side areas, sow earlier, and choose very hardy options like cereal rye.

    Scatter the seed over loosened soil, rake it in lightly, and water if the fall rains haven't started. Covering seed with a thin layer of soil also keeps birds from eating it.

    How much seed, and how to sow it

    Cover crop seed is sold by the pound at farm stores and many garden centers, and some seed companies sell small bags of mixes for home gardens. Seeding rates are printed on the bag, and it's worth following them, but as a rough guide small-seeded crops like crimson clover go on thinly, a few ounces per 100 square feet, while large seeds like favas and peas need several times that by weight. A 4 by 8 foot raised bed doesn't need much of anything.

    Legume seed often comes with, or can be bought with, an "inoculant": a powder containing the soil bacteria that form nodules on legume roots and capture nitrogen from the air. In beds where peas, beans or clover have grown before, the bacteria are usually already there. In a new bed, inoculating is cheap insurance. Dampen the seed slightly, toss it with the powder and sow right away.

    How much nitrogen do you really get?

    Legumes don't make nitrogen from nothing. The bacteria in their root nodules pull it from the air, and the plant stores it in its leaves and stems. If you dig up a healthy fava or pea plant in spring, the little pink nodules on the roots are a good sign the bacteria are working. Most of the nitrogen is in the green growth, not the roots, so it only reaches next year's crops if you turn the tops back into the soil or compost them. Let the plants grow as long as you can before cutting them; a cover crop cut in February has much less to give than one cut in April.

    Turning it under in spring

    Cut or turn the cover crop under in spring, ideally when legumes start to flower and before any of it sets seed. Chop it with a spade or cut it at the base, then dig or fork it into the top few inches of soil. Give it two to three weeks to break down before planting. Fresh green material can tie up nitrogen and get in the way of small seeds for a short time, and rotting plant matter attracts seedcorn maggots, which tunnel into bean, corn and squash seeds.

    If digging a whole bed sounds like too much work, there's a no-dig option. Cut the cover crop at ground level, leave the cut plants lying on the surface as mulch, and plant transplants through it. This works best with crops that don't regrow after cutting, like favas and peas cut in flower. Cereal rye regrows if cut too early, so cut it once it has headed out, or pull it.

    Be ready to deal with it on time. Cereal rye left too long gets chest-high and woody, and crimson clover that goes to seed will come back as a weed. In a wet Northwest spring the soil can stay too soggy to dig for weeks, so many gardeners cut their cover crop in March or early April and let it lie, then dig it in once the soil dries.

    No room for a cover crop? A thick layer of shredded leaves or straw over the bed also protects the soil through winter. Garlic, which goes in each fall, can share space with mulch too. See how to plant garlic.
