    How Much to Water Vegetables in a Dry Northwest Summer

    Northwest summers are close to rainless from July to September. How much vegetables need each week, and drip, soaker hose or hand watering.

    EHBy Elaine Holloway
    Posted October 2026 · Updated November 2026

    The short answer

    How much
    About an inch a week, rain included; more in heat, sandy soil and wind.
    How
    Deep soaks once or twice a week, at the base of the plants, in the morning.
    Save water
    2 to 3 inches of mulch once the soil has warmed.

    If you've just moved to the Northwest, summer is a surprise. You spend November to May wondering if it will ever stop raining, and then sometime in early July it just does. July and August are usually among the driest months of the year, west of the Cascades and even more so east of them, and your vegetables are growing their fastest exactly when the rain disappears.

    If you've gardened here a while, you probably know the other side of it too: the tomato that cracked after a heavy soak, the lettuce that bolted in a week of heat, the squash leaves flopping at 4 p.m. and perking up by morning. Most of those come down to how, and how evenly, the garden gets watered.

    How much

    A common rule of thumb is about an inch of water a week for an established vegetable garden, from rain and watering combined. Hot spells, sandy soil, windy sites and the dry east side all push that number up. Large, fruiting plants like squash and tomatoes drink more than small leafy crops.

    Don't rely on the rule alone. Push a finger or a trowel two or three inches into the soil next to your plants. If it's dry at that depth, it's time to water.

    What does "an inch" look like?

    Most of us have no idea how much water our sprinkler or hose actually puts down, and it's easy to find out. Set a few empty tuna cans or other straight-sided containers around the bed, run your sprinkler as usual, and time how long it takes for them to fill to an inch. You may be surprised: many people find their usual 15-minute evening watering puts down only a fraction of that.

    For soaker hoses and drip lines, the can trick doesn't work, so check the soil instead. Water as usual, wait an hour, then dig a small hole a few inches from a plant. If the soil is moist 6 inches down, you watered deeply enough. If it's dry below the top inch or two, run it longer next time. Once you know, write the time down, and you won't have to guess again.

    As a rough guide, an inch of water over a 4 by 8 foot bed is about 20 gallons.

    Water deeply, less often

    A long soak once or twice a week is better than a quick sprinkle every day. Deep watering pushes moisture down where roots can follow it, so plants handle hot days better. Light daily watering keeps the roots near the surface, where they dry out first.

    Seeds and newly planted seedlings are the exception. Keep the top inch moist until they're established, which can mean watering every day in warm weather.

    Water at the base, in the morning

    • Soaker hoses and drip lines put water right at the roots, waste very little to evaporation and keep leaves dry. Keeping leaves dry helps prevent diseases like powdery mildew and tomato blight. Add a simple timer and the garden nearly waters itself.
    • Morning is the best time. Less water evaporates than at midday, and any splashed leaves dry quickly.
    • If you hand-water, aim at the soil, not the leaves, and go slowly so the water soaks in instead of running off.

    Mulch keeps the water in

    Once the soil has warmed in late spring, spread 2 to 3 inches of straw, shredded leaves or compost around your plants. Mulch slows evaporation, keeps the soil cooler and more even, and smothers weeds that would compete for water. It's the single cheapest way to cut your watering.

    Crop by crop

    • Tomatoes: keep watering even, since swings between dry and soaked can cause cracked fruit and blossom end rot. Ease off a little in late summer to encourage ripening.
    • Squash and cucumbers: heavy drinkers once they're fruiting. Bitter cucumbers are often a sign of drought stress.
    • Beans: water is most important when they're flowering and setting pods.
    • Lettuce and leafy greens: shallow roots need frequent water, and drought stress makes them bolt and turn bitter.

    Reading your plants

    Plants tell you a lot if you look at the right time of day. Big-leaved crops like squash, cucumbers and even tomatoes often droop in hot afternoon sun even when the soil is moist; the leaves are just losing water faster than the roots can take it up. If they've perked up by the next morning, they're fine. If they're still drooping at breakfast time, they need water.

    Overwatering is less common in summer but it does happen, especially in heavy clay soil or pots without good drainage. Yellowing lower leaves, soil that stays soggy for days, and plants that wilt even though the soil is wet all point to roots that are short of air. Let the soil dry out a bit between waterings.

    Pots and containers

    Containers are the exception to "water less often." The small amount of soil in a pot dries out quickly, and in a July heat wave a tomato in a pot may need water every day, sometimes twice. Water until it runs out the bottom. Bigger pots dry out more slowly, so go for the largest you have room for, and group pots together so they shade each other. Our container gardening guide has more.

    When a heat wave hits

    Northwest heat waves can push temperatures well past 100°F, even west of the Cascades. Before one arrives, give the whole garden a deep soak and top up the mulch. Water early in the morning during the hot spell, not in the heat of the afternoon. Shade cloth, an old sheet on stakes, or even a patio umbrella can protect lettuce, young transplants and anything newly planted from the worst of the afternoon sun. Expect tomatoes and peppers to drop some flowers when it's very hot; they'll set fruit again when it cools down.

    Watering is one job in the monthly garden calendar, along with everything else to do each summer month.
