    Starting Vegetable Seeds Indoors: A Northwest Timeline

    Onions in late winter, peppers next, tomatoes last. A Northwest seed-starting timeline and the light setup that keeps seedlings stocky.

    By Jigmey Dorjee
    Posted October 2026 · Updated November 2026

    The short answer

    When
    Count back from your planting-out date, not the last frost, and don't start too early.
    Worth it
    Tomatoes, peppers, eggplant, onions and leeks. Sow root crops, peas and beans outside.
    Light
    LED shop lights a few inches above the seedlings, 14 to 16 hours a day.

    Some crops need more time than a Northwest summer gives them. Tomatoes, peppers and eggplant are the big three: if you sow them outside, they won't ripen before fall. Starting seeds indoors gives them a head start of six to ten weeks, and it opens up hundreds of varieties you'll never find as nursery starts.

    When to start

    Count back from the date you'll plant outside, not from the last frost. Rainline Garden works this out for each town. For Portland:

    • Peppers: start indoors Mar 16–30, plant out May 25 – Jun 15.
    • Tomatoes: start indoors Mar 20 – Apr 3, plant out May 15 – Jun 12.
    • Broccoli: start indoors Jan 25 – Feb 8, plant out Mar 15 – Apr 19.

    Other towns shift a lot. Tomatoes start indoors Mar 20 – Apr 3 in Seattle and Apr 14–28 in Bend. Check your own dates on the planner, or build a dated list with My garden plan.

    Starting too early is the most common mistake. Seedlings that wait too long indoors get tall, weak and rootbound. It's hard to resist when you're itching to garden in February, but a tomato started in early April and planted out in May will usually outgrow one started in February that sat in a pot for three months.

    What's worth starting yourself (and what isn't)

    Not everything needs a head start. Tomatoes, peppers, eggplant, onions and leeks benefit the most, because they're slow. Broccoli, cabbage, cauliflower, kale and lettuce are easy to start indoors and give you a few weeks' jump on slugs. Squash, cucumbers and melons can be started indoors, but only about three to four weeks ahead; their roots resent being disturbed, so sow them in individual pots.

    Some crops are better sown straight into the garden. Carrots, beets, radishes, parsnips and other root crops don't transplant well, and the roots fork or stunt if moved. Peas, beans and corn grow so fast from seed outdoors that starting them indoors rarely gains much.

    If you only have room for one tray, fill it with tomatoes and peppers. Those are the varieties where nursery choice is most limited, and where starting your own lets you pick ones bred for short, cool seasons.

    What you need

    • Seed-starting mix. It's lighter and cleaner than garden soil or regular potting soil, which helps prevent seedling diseases.
    • Containers with drainage. Cell trays, small pots or recycled yogurt cups with holes punched in the bottom all work.
    • Light. A sunny Northwest window in March isn't enough, and seedlings stretch toward it. Simple LED shop lights hung a few inches above the seedlings, on for about 14 to 16 hours a day, grow far sturdier plants. Raise the lights as the plants grow.
    • Warmth for sprouting. Tomatoes and especially peppers sprout faster in warm soil. A seedling heat mat helps a lot in a cool house. Take trays off the mat once most seeds are up.

    Sowing and growing on

    1. Moisten the mix before filling containers. It should feel like a wrung-out sponge.
    2. Sow two or three seeds per cell at the depth on the packet, usually two to three times the seed's width.
    3. Cover with a clear lid or plastic wrap until seeds sprout, then remove it so air can move.
    4. Thin to the strongest seedling by snipping the others with scissors.
    5. Water from the bottom when the surface feels dry, and keep a small fan nearby on low. Moving air makes stems stronger and helps prevent damping off, a fungus that makes seedlings collapse at the soil line.
    6. Move tomatoes and peppers to bigger pots when they have a couple of true leaves. Tomatoes can be buried up to their first leaves; they'll grow roots along the buried stem.

    Feeding seedlings

    Seed-starting mix contains very little fertilizer, which is good for sprouting but means seedlings start to go hungry after a few weeks. Once they have their first true leaves (the second set of leaves, which look like the plant's real leaves), water them every week or so with a liquid fertilizer mixed at half the label strength. When you move them up to bigger pots, use a potting mix that contains some fertilizer.

    When seedlings don't look right

    Tall, pale and floppy almost always means not enough light. Lower the lights so they're just a few inches above the leaves, or add a second light. Leggy tomatoes can be rescued by burying the stem deeper when you transplant them. Leggy peppers and brassicas can't, so it's better to prevent it.

    Collapsing at the soil line is damping off. Once a seedling falls over, it won't recover. Remove the affected ones, let the surface dry out a bit between waterings, keep the fan running, and next time start with fresh mix and clean containers.

    Yellowing lower leaves after a few weeks usually means they need food. Purple undersides on tomato leaves often mean the room or the roots are too cold. White, crispy patches after a day outdoors are sunscald from skipping hardening off; the plant usually grows past it.

    Keep a simple note of when you sowed, when they came up, and when you planted out. By your second or third year, you'll have your own timeline that fits your house and your garden better than any chart.

    Harden them off

    Seedlings raised indoors have never felt sun, wind or a cool night. About a week before planting out, set them outside in a sheltered spot for an hour or two, then a little longer each day, until they're out all day and night. Skipping this step is how good seedlings get scorched or stunted in their first week in the garden. The glossary has more.
