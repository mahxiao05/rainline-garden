    When and How to Plant Garlic

    Garlic goes in around October and comes out the next July. Planting dates by region, hardneck or softneck, and curing the harvest.

    By Jigmey Dorjee
    Posted October 2026 · Updated November 2026

    The short answer

    When
    In fall, a few weeks before the ground freezes. Around Portland: Oct 1 – Nov 15.
    How
    Cloves pointed end up, 2 inches deep and 6 inches apart, then a few inches of mulch.
    Harvest
    Next summer, once the lower third of the leaves have turned brown.

    Garlic is one of the easiest and most rewarding crops in a Northwest garden, and it runs on a different schedule from almost everything else. You plant it in fall, it grows roots and a few leaves before winter, sits through the cold, and then bulks up in spring. By early summer you're digging heads of garlic out of a bed you haven't thought about since October.

    It's also one of the few crops where the hardest part is remembering to do it. Most of us think about the garden in spring, and by then it's too late for a full-sized garlic harvest. If you're reading this in the fall, you're right on time.

    How much to plant

    Each clove you plant grows into one whole head. So count backward from how much garlic you use. If you go through a head a week, that's about 50 heads a year, plus some extra to replant next fall. A typical head breaks into 6 to 10 planting cloves depending on the variety, so a pound of seed garlic usually plants somewhere around 40 to 80 cloves. At 6 inches apart, a 4 by 8 foot bed holds roughly 100 to 120 plants.

    When to plant

    Plant garlic in fall, after the soil has cooled but a few weeks before it freezes hard, so the cloves can root in. That timing varies a lot across the region:

    • Portland and the west side: Oct 1 – Nov 15. See Portland's garlic page.
    • Spokane and the inland Northwest: Sep 25 – Oct 31.
    • Boise and the Treasure Valley: Oct 1 – Nov 1.
    • Bend and the high desert: Sep 20 – Oct 20.

    If you miss the window, you can still plant in early spring as soon as the soil can be worked, but the heads will be smaller.

    Hardneck or softneck?

    Hardneck garlic grows a stiff center stalk and sends up a curly flower stem called a scape in early summer. The cloves are bigger and easier to peel, and it handles cold winters well. Softneck garlic has no stiff stalk, stores longer and is the kind you can braid. Both grow well west of the Cascades; hardnecks are the safer choice where winters are cold.

    Within each group there are dozens of varieties, and part of the fun is trying a few. Porcelain hardnecks such as Music and German Extra Hardy have big, easy-to-peel cloves, usually four to six per head. Rocambole types are known for rich flavor but don't store as long. Among softnecks, Inchelium Red, a heirloom found on the Colville Reservation in northeastern Washington, is a popular Northwest choice that stores well. Silverskins keep the longest of all. Plant two or three varieties side by side and you'll soon find the ones you like best in your soil.

    Buy seed garlic from a garden center or seed company rather than planting grocery-store bulbs. Store garlic is often a variety grown for a mild climate, and it may have been treated to slow sprouting.

    How to plant

    1. Pick a sunny spot with soil that drains. Garlic rots in soggy ground, so a raised bed is ideal in rainy areas.
    2. Loosen the soil and mix in an inch or two of compost.
    3. Break the heads into cloves just before planting. Save the biggest cloves for planting; they grow the biggest heads.
    4. Plant each clove pointed end up, about 2 inches deep and 6 inches apart.
    5. Cover the bed with a few inches of straw or shredded leaves. Mulch keeps the soil from heaving in freeze-thaw cycles and smothers spring weeds.

    Spring and early summer care

    When green shoots start growing strongly in late winter or early spring, garlic benefits from a little nitrogen. Side-dress with a nitrogen fertilizer or a dose of blood meal or feather meal once in early spring and again a few weeks later. Stop feeding by about mid-May, when the bulbs start to swell; late fertilizer delays bulbing and can hurt storage.

    Pull weeds as they appear, because garlic doesn't compete well. Water during dry spells in spring. On hardneck garlic, snap off the scapes when they curl; they're delicious sautéed, and removing them helps the plant put its energy into the bulb. Stop watering once the leaves start to yellow in early summer.

    Harvesting and curing

    Dig garlic when the lower third or so of the leaves have turned brown but the upper leaves are still green. Each green leaf is a wrapper around the bulb, so waiting too long leaves the heads with thin skins that split. Loosen the soil with a garden fork and lift the plants rather than pulling them.

    Cure the whole plants in a shady, dry, airy place for two to three weeks, then trim the roots and stems. Keep the biggest, best heads to plant again in fall.

    Once cured, store garlic somewhere cool, dark and dry with some air movement, not in the fridge and not in a sealed bag. Softnecks usually keep the longest, often six months or more. Hardnecks generally keep for a few months, so eat those first and save the softnecks for late winter.

    Problems to watch for

    Rust shows up as small orange spots on the leaves, usually in late spring. It's common in wet Northwest springs. Light cases rarely do much harm; in bad years it can shrink the bulbs. Space plants for airflow, water at the soil rather than over the leaves, and don't plant garlic or onions in the same spot every year.

    White rot is much more serious. Plants yellow and wilt from the outer leaves in, and the base of the bulb is covered with fluffy white fungus and tiny black specks. Those specks can survive in the soil for many years, which makes the bed unusable for garlic, onions and leeks. The best protection is not bringing it in: buy seed garlic from a reputable source, and never plant cloves from a crop that showed these signs. If you find it, remove the plants with the soil around them and put them in the trash.

    Onion maggots and thrips sometimes bother garlic too, though usually less than they bother onions. Row cover in spring and rotating where you plant help with both.
