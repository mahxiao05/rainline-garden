    How to Control Slugs in a Northwest Garden

    Practical, pet-safe ways to protect seedlings, lettuce and strawberries from the Pacific Northwest's most famous garden pest.

    EHBy Elaine Holloway
    Posted October 2026 · Updated November 2026

    On this page:Meet the slugs (and spare the big yellow one)Know when they strikeIs it really slugs?What worksMake the garden less welcomingWhat doesn't work as well as you'd hopeA slug calendar for the Northwest

    You sow a row of lettuce on a mild April afternoon. A few days later, little green loops are showing all down the row. The next morning half of them are gone, and all that's left is a few stubs and a silver trail glinting across the soil. If you garden west of the Cascades, you've probably lived some version of this.

    The mild, damp climate that makes the Northwest so green suits slugs perfectly, and they're most active exactly when your seedlings are smallest: on cool, wet spring nights. You won't get rid of them. But once you know which slugs you're dealing with and when they breed, you can keep the damage small with surprisingly little effort.

    Meet the slugs (and spare the big yellow one)

    Not every slug in your garden is a problem. The one doing most of the damage is usually the gray garden slug: small, often about an inch long, light gray to tan and sometimes mottled. It came from Europe, breeds fast, and eats seedlings, lettuce, strawberries and almost anything soft. Larger European slugs, some brown, black or orange, turn up in gardens too and eat plenty.

    The banana slug, the big yellow-green one you see on forest trails, is native to the Northwest. It mostly eats fallen leaves, fungi and rotting plant matter and is rarely the culprit in a vegetable bed. If you find one in the garden, move it to a shady corner or the compost pile and let it do its job.

    Know when they strike

    Slugs need moisture. They breathe through their skin and dry out in sun and wind, so they feed at night and on overcast, drizzly days, then hide under boards, mulch, pots, dense groundcovers and the lower leaves of lettuce and strawberries. They go after tender seedlings, lettuce, beans, strawberries (often a hollow pit in a ripe berry) and dahlia shoots first. Damage shows up as ragged holes in leaves and shiny slime trails. A seedling can vanish overnight.

    They also breed on the Northwest's schedule. Gray garden slugs lay clusters of small, round, pearly eggs in damp soil and under debris, mostly in fall and again in spring when the rain is steady, and one slug can lay hundreds of eggs in a season. Eggs laid in fall hatch over winter and early spring, right when you start planting. That's why damage in April often feels sudden: a new generation has just hatched under your mulch.

    Is it really slugs?

    Holes in leaves have other causes too, so check before you start baiting. Slime trails and damage overnight point to slugs or snails. Lots of tiny round pits in young leaves of radishes, arugula or eggplant are flea beetles. Ragged holes with small green droppings in kale or broccoli are caterpillars; see cabbage worms and aphids. Earwigs chew irregular holes at night too, and often hide in dahlia and lettuce leaves by day. A flashlight walk an hour after dark settles it: you'll see who's eating.

    What works

    Iron phosphate bait is the go-to choice. It's sold under several brand names and is considered safe around pets, wildlife and food crops when used as directed. It works more slowly than people expect: slugs that eat it stop feeding within hours, then crawl off and die over the next several days, usually out of sight. So don't judge it by whether you find bodies. Judge it by whether your seedlings survive. Scatter it lightly around plants, a thin sprinkle rather than a pile, after rain or watering, when slugs are active. Avoid older baits containing metaldehyde, which are toxic to dogs and cats.

    Hand-picking is surprisingly effective in a small garden. Head out with a flashlight an hour after dark on a damp night, or lay a few boards on the soil and check underneath them each morning. Drop what you find into a jar of soapy water.

    Beer traps attract and drown slugs. Sink a shallow container so its rim sits about half an inch above the soil, which keeps helpful ground beetles from falling in, and fill it with cheap beer. Empty and refill it every few days.

    Bigger transplants survive better. A lettuce or bean plant started indoors and set out with several true leaves can lose a few bites and keep growing. A seedling that has just come up can't.

    Copper tape around the top edge of raised beds and containers discourages slugs from climbing in. It's most useful on pots and beds that are already slug-free inside.

    Make the garden less welcoming

    Water in the morning instead of the evening, so the soil surface is drier at night. Keep a strip of bare ground around beds, and hold off on thick mulch until the soil warms in late spring: a cool mulch layer in April is slug housing. Clear out boards, weeds and debris where slugs hide during the day. And encourage their predators. Ground beetles, garter snakes, frogs and birds all eat slugs, and a bit of leaf litter or a rock pile away from the vegetable beds gives them somewhere to live.

    What doesn't work as well as you'd hope

    • Eggshells and coffee grounds. Popular, but slugs cross them without much trouble, especially once rain softens them.
    • Diatomaceous earth. It works only while dry, which in a Northwest spring is rarely for long.
    • Salt. It kills slugs, but it also damages your soil and plants. Keep it out of the garden.

    A slug calendar for the Northwest

    1. Late winter: lay a few boards or upturned pots near the beds and check under them each morning. You'll catch the first slugs before they breed again.
    2. Spring planting: sprinkle iron phosphate bait lightly around the bed the day you sow or transplant. Refresh it after heavy rain, and check seedlings on damp evenings.
    3. Late spring: once plants have a few sets of true leaves, they can usually outgrow light damage, and you can ease off.
    4. Summer: slugs hide in dry weather. Keep up morning watering, and check under strawberry leaves.
    5. Late summer and fall: when the rain returns, slugs come back and lay a big batch of eggs. Bait around fall crops and clear debris where eggs would be laid. Every slug you remove in October is one fewer laying eggs that hatch in April.

    Spring is the critical time. Start checking and baiting as soon as you sow, especially for lettuce and beans.
