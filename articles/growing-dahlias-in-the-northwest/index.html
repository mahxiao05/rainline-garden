    Growing Dahlias

    Dahlia tubers rot in cold, wet soil, so timing matters more than anything. Planting, staking, and digging or mulching in winter.

    EHBy Elaine Holloway
    Posted October 2026 · Updated November 2026

    The short answer

    When
    Once the soil has warmed, about when you'd plant tomatoes.
    How
    Tubers 4 to 6 inches deep on their side, stake in first, and no water until shoots appear.
    Winter
    West side: leave them in well-drained ground under mulch. East side: dig them after the first frost.

    Dahlias are some of the most popular flowers in Northwest gardens, and for good reason. Our mild summers suit them, and they bloom from midsummer until the first frost. They come in nearly every color and in sizes from small pompons to blooms as wide as a dinner plate.

    Fair warning: they're addictive. Most people start with three or four tubers and find themselves, a few years later, with a whole bed of them and a box of labeled tubers in the garage every winter. A single plant can give you armfuls of cut flowers from July to October, which is a lot of flowers for the price of one tuber.

    Picking a type

    Catalogs sort dahlias by flower form, and it helps to know the main ones. Ball and pompon dahlias are tight, round globes, great for cutting. Decorative dahlias have broad, flat petals and range from small to the giant "dinnerplate" types. Cactus and semi-cactus dahlias have narrow, pointed, rolled petals that give a spiky look. Single, collarette and anemone types have an open center, which bees can reach.

    The giant dinnerplates are showstoppers but need sturdy staking and tend to produce fewer flowers. Smaller ball, pompon and decorative types usually bloom more, with longer stems for cutting. If you want a lot of flowers for the house, start there.

    When to plant

    Dahlias grow from tubers, which rot easily in cold, wet soil. Wait until the soil has warmed, about the same time you'd plant tomatoes. Rainline Garden's planting windows:

    • Portland and the west side: May 15 – Jun 12. See Portland's dahlia page.
    • Spokane and the inland Northwest: May 20 – Jun 17.
    • Bend and the high desert: Jun 9 – Jul 7.

    If you'd like flowers a few weeks earlier, you can start tubers in pots indoors or in a greenhouse about a month before planting time. Use a pot just big enough for the tuber, barely moist potting mix, and a bright spot. Plant them out, sprouts and all, once the soil has warmed. Harden them off first, just as you would vegetable seedlings.

    How to plant

    1. Pick a sunny spot. Dahlias need at least six hours of sun and bloom best with more.
    2. Make sure it drains. In heavy clay, plant in a raised bed or mix in plenty of compost.
    3. Put in a stake first for varieties that grow over about 3 feet tall. Placing it now avoids spearing the tuber later.
    4. Plant the tuber 4 to 6 inches deep, lying on its side with the old stem or any sprouting eyes facing up. Space plants about 18 inches to 2 feet apart, more for the biggest varieties.
    5. Hold off on water. Unless the soil is very dry, don't water until shoots appear. A wet tuber with no leaves to use the water is the one most likely to rot.

    Summer care

    • Protect the shoots from slugs. Young dahlia shoots are a favorite. See dealing with slugs.
    • Pinch for more flowers. When the plant is about a foot tall, pinch out the center tip just above a set of leaves. It branches out and produces more stems.
    • Water deeply once plants are growing, about once or twice a week in dry weather.
    • Tie stems to the stake as they grow.
    • Cut often. Cutting flowers and removing spent blooms keeps new buds coming. Cut in the cool of the morning, and pick blooms that are almost fully open, since dahlias don't open much more once cut.

    Pests and problems

    Besides slugs, the most common dahlia pest in the Northwest is the earwig, which chews ragged holes in petals at night and hides inside the flowers by day. Rolled-up newspaper or a short piece of hose laid near the plants traps them; shake them out each morning. In hot, dry spells, especially east of the Cascades, spider mites can make leaves look dull and speckled, with fine webbing underneath. A strong spray of water under the leaves helps. Powdery mildew often coats the leaves late in the season. It looks bad but rarely does much harm that late; give plants space for airflow.

    Making cut flowers last

    Dahlias don't last as long in a vase as some flowers, often several days, but you can help. Cut in the cool of the morning, choosing blooms that are almost fully open. Strip the lower leaves and put the stems straight into water. Many growers then stand the cut ends in a few inches of hot water for about an hour before arranging them, which seems to help the stems take up water. Change the vase water every day or two.

    What to do in winter

    A frost blackens dahlia foliage and ends the season. What comes next depends on where you live.

    • West of the Cascades: in well-drained soil, many gardeners cut the stems down and leave the tubers in the ground under a thick layer of mulch. In heavy or soggy soil, they often rot over a wet winter, so digging them up is safer.
    • East of the Cascades: winters are too cold for tubers in the ground. Dig them after the first frost.

    To store dug tubers, shake off the soil, let them dry for a few days somewhere sheltered, then pack them in slightly damp wood shavings, peat or vermiculite. Keep them somewhere cool but frost-free, around 40 to 50°F, and check them during winter for any that are rotting or shriveling.

    In spring, you can divide large clumps to make more plants. Each piece needs part of the old stem's base (the crown) with at least one eye, a small bump where the new shoot will grow. A tuber without an eye won't sprout, no matter how plump it is. Eyes are easier to see in spring, once they start to swell, than in fall. Use a clean, sharp knife, let the cut surfaces dry for a day, and plant as usual. A single clump often divides into several plants, which is how a few tubers turn into a whole bed.

    Label everything. Every dahlia grower has a box of mystery tubers in the garage. A marker on each tuber, or a tag in each bag, saves a lot of guessing next spring.

    Dahlias also feed bees late in the season, especially the open-centered single types. For more on that, see planting for pollinators.
