    Growing Blueberries

    Acidic, rainy west-side soil is close to what blueberries want. Choosing two varieties that pollinate each other, planting, and pruning.

    EHBy Elaine Holloway
    Posted October 2026 · Updated November 2026

    The short answer

    Soil
    Acidic, about pH 4.5 to 5.5, well drained, with 2 to 3 inches of wood mulch.
    Plant
    Two or more varieties, 4 to 5 feet apart in full sun, in fall or early spring.
    Pick
    A few days after berries turn blue, when they roll off into your hand.

    If there's one fruit made for Northwest gardens, it's the blueberry. Many of our soils are naturally acidic, winters are mild enough west of the Cascades, and summer brings the long, bright days the berries need to ripen. Oregon and Washington are among the country's biggest blueberry-growing states for good reason. A few well-placed bushes can feed a family for 30 years or more.

    Get the soil right first

    Blueberries need acidic soil, ideally with a pH of about 4.5 to 5.5. That's more acidic than most vegetables like. A soil test tells you where you're starting; many west-side soils are already close, while east-side soils are often too alkaline and need more work or a raised bed.

    • Mix peat moss or acidic compost into a wide planting area, not just the hole.
    • If the pH is too high, elemental sulfur lowers it slowly. Add it months ahead of planting if you can, and follow the amount your soil test suggests.
    • Blueberry roots are shallow and fine, and they rot in standing water. In heavy clay, plant in a raised bed or a broad mound.

    Pick two or more varieties

    Northern highbush blueberries, the type grown across the Northwest, can set fruit on their own, but planting two or more varieties that bloom around the same time usually gives you bigger berries and more of them. Mixing early, mid and late varieties also stretches the harvest over the whole summer. Good choices here include:

    • Duke, an early variety.
    • Bluecrop, a dependable midseason standard.
    • Chandler, a late variety known for very large berries.

    Planting and mulch

    Plant in fall or early spring, in full sun, about 4 to 5 feet apart. Set the plant at the same depth it grew in the pot. Then mulch with 2 to 3 inches of sawdust, bark or wood chips and keep that mulch topped up every year. It holds moisture around the shallow roots, keeps weeds down, and slowly adds organic matter.

    For the first year, pinch off the flowers. It feels wasteful, but it lets the plant build roots and branches, and you'll get much bigger harvests in the years that follow.

    Water, feed and protect

    • Water through the dry summer. Shallow roots dry out fast. Drip irrigation or a soaker hose under the mulch is ideal.
    • Feed lightly in spring with a fertilizer made for acid-loving plants such as rhododendrons and azaleas. Blueberries take up nitrogen best in the ammonium form found in these fertilizers (ammonium sulfate is the classic one), and too much fertilizer of any kind can burn their fine roots. Split the amount into two or three small feedings between bud break and early June rather than one big one.
    • Net the bushes as berries start to color. Birds find ripe blueberries fast.

    Pruning

    After the first few years, prune in late winter while the plants are dormant. Remove dead or damaged wood, crossing branches and a few of the oldest, thickest canes at the base. Younger canes produce the best fruit, so the goal is a steady supply of new ones.

    A useful rule of thumb from university fruit guides: on a mature bush, canes older than about six years produce less and smaller fruit. Each winter, cut one or two of the oldest canes right down to the ground, and keep a few strong new ones that come up from the base to replace them. Thin out short, twiggy growth in the center so light gets into the bush. Fruit forms on last year's growth, so the plump, rounded buds near branch tips are next summer's flowers; the small, pointed buds are leaf buds.

    Picking: wait a few more days

    The most common harvest mistake is picking too soon. A berry turns fully blue several days before it reaches full sweetness, and the flavor keeps improving while it stays on the bush. Look for berries with no red or green left around the stem, and roll a cluster gently with your thumb: ripe berries drop into your hand and unripe ones hang on. Pick every few days through the season, since a bush ripens its berries gradually over several weeks rather than all at once. With early, mid and late varieties planted together, picking can run from early summer well into August west of the Cascades.

    Common problems

    • Yellow leaves with green veins. Usually a sign that the soil pH is too high, so the plant can't take up iron. It's common in east-side and alkaline soils. A soil test confirms it; sulfur and an acidic mulch fix it slowly.
    • Shriveled, gray, hard berries. This is often mummy berry, a fungal disease that's common in wet Northwest springs. The mummified berries fall and carry the fungus over winter. Rake them up or bury them under fresh mulch before spring, and remove any you see on the bush.
    • Soft, leaking berries with tiny holes. Spotted wing drosophila, a small fruit fly that arrived in the Northwest around 2009, lays eggs in ripening fruit. Pick often and promptly, don't leave overripe or fallen berries lying around, and refrigerate what you pick.
    • Few berries on a healthy-looking bush. Too much shade, too much nitrogen, heavy pruning that removed last year's growth, or a young plant that just needs another year or two.

    Growing blueberries in pots

    If your soil is alkaline, heavy clay or just full of tree roots, a large container works well. Use a pot of at least 15 to 20 gallons (a half barrel is ideal) and fill it with a potting mix made for acid-loving plants, or one with plenty of peat or bark. Compact varieties bred for containers stay smaller, but a standard highbush will also grow in a big pot for years. Container plants dry out fast in summer, so water often, and in a hard freeze move the pot against the house or wrap it, since roots in a pot are much less protected than roots in the ground.
