    Growing Strawberries

    June-bearers give one big crop, everbearers a trickle all summer. Planting, mulching against rot, and renewing an old patch.

    EHBy Elaine Holloway
    Posted October 2026 · Updated November 2026

    The short answer

    Which type
    June-bearing for one big crop; day-neutral for smaller crops into fall.
    Plant
    Early spring, full sun, good drainage, with the crown right at the soil surface.
    Watch out
    Gray mold in wet springs. Straw under the plants and frequent picking help.

    Northwest strawberries have a reputation, and they've earned it. The mild summers and cool nights produce berries with deep flavor, and Northwest plant breeders have developed varieties suited to this climate for decades. A small patch is easy to start and, with a little care, keeps producing for several years.

    Choose your type

    • June-bearing plants produce one big crop over a few weeks in early summer. They're the classic choice for jam and freezing. Hood, an older Oregon-bred variety, is famous for flavor; Shuksan and Tillamook are other Northwest-bred options.
    • Day-neutral plants, often sold as everbearing, produce smaller flushes from early summer into fall. Albion and Seascape are common choices. They're good for snacking and for small spaces like containers.

    Many gardeners grow some of each: June-bearers for a big harvest and day-neutrals to keep berries coming.

    Planting

    Plant in early spring, in full sun, in soil that drains well. Strawberries hate wet feet, so raised beds or raised rows are a big help in rainy areas. Space plants about 12 to 18 inches apart.

    Depth matters more than with most plants. Set each plant so the crown, the thick point where the leaves meet the roots, sits right at the soil surface. Buried crowns rot; crowns set too high dry out.

    Most strawberries are sold two ways: as bare-root bundles in late winter and early spring, or as potted plants later on. Bare-root plants are cheaper and establish well if you plant them promptly. Soak the roots in a bucket of water for an hour, trim very long roots back to about 5 inches so they don't bend upward in the hole, and spread them out in a fan before you firm the soil around them. Potted plants are easier to handle and can go in later, but cost more.

    Avoid planting where tomatoes, potatoes, peppers or eggplant grew in the last few years. Those crops can carry verticillium wilt, a soil-borne disease that also attacks strawberries and makes plants wilt and collapse in summer.

    The first year

    On June-bearers, pinch off all the flowers the first year. On day-neutrals, pinch flowers until about early July, then let them fruit. Both feel painful, but plants that put their first season into roots and leaves produce far more afterward.

    Mulch and keep the berries clean

    Gray mold, a fuzzy gray rot on the berries, is the main strawberry problem in wet Northwest springs. A layer of straw under the plants keeps berries off the soil and dries out faster after rain. Pick ripe berries every couple of days, remove any moldy ones from the patch, and give plants enough space for air to move through.

    Slugs love strawberries too. Check our guide to Northwest slugs for safe ways to control them.

    Picking for the best flavor

    Pick berries when they're red all over, including the shoulders near the cap. Unlike some fruit, strawberries don't keep sweetening after they're picked. Harvest in the cool of the morning, pinch the stem rather than pulling the berry, and leave the green cap on, which helps them keep. Don't wash them until you're about to eat them. A June-bearing patch usually ripens over two to three weeks, so pick every day or two at the peak.

    Other pests and problems

    • Notched leaf edges and plants that wilt or stop growing. Root weevils are common in Northwest gardens. The adults chew notches in leaf edges at night, while the grubs eat roots underground. Beneficial nematodes applied to moist soil in spring or late summer can help, and a badly damaged patch is best replaced in a new spot.
    • Soft, collapsing berries in late summer. Spotted wing drosophila, a small fruit fly, lays eggs in ripening fruit. It hits day-neutral varieties more than June-bearers because they fruit later in the season. Pick often and don't leave overripe berries on the plants.
    • Birds. A light net over hoops keeps robins and other birds off when berries start to color.

    Runners and renewing the patch

    Strawberries send out runners, long stems that root into new plants. Let some fill gaps in the row, but cut most of them off so the bed doesn't turn into a crowded mat. After June-bearers finish fruiting, many gardeners renew the bed: mow or cut the old leaves, thin out crowded plants, weed, and feed lightly so the plants regrow for next year.

    Even well-kept patches slow down after three or four years. Start a fresh bed in a new spot with healthy new plants, and the cycle continues.

    Winter care

    West of the Cascades, strawberries usually need little help in winter beyond good drainage. In colder areas east of the Cascades and in the high desert, cover the plants with a few inches of loose straw once they've gone dormant after several hard frosts in late fall. It protects the crowns from deep cold and from freezing and thawing that can push them out of the ground. Pull the straw back into the paths in spring when new leaves start to grow, and keep some handy to throw back over the plants if a late frost is forecast while they're blooming.

    Strawberries in containers

    Day-neutral varieties do well in pots, window boxes and hanging baskets, which keep the berries off the ground and away from many slugs. Use a pot at least 8 inches deep with good drainage, a quality potting mix, and two or three plants per foot of space. Containers dry out fast in summer, so water often, and feed lightly every few weeks while they're fruiting, since frequent watering washes nutrients out.
