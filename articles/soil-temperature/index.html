    Why Northwest Gardeners Should Plant by Soil Temperature

    Beans and squash rot in cold soil even after the last frost. A cheap soil thermometer settles when it's warm enough to plant.

    EHBy Elaine Holloway
    Posted October 2026 · Updated November 2026

    The short answer

    Cool crops
    Peas, spinach and lettuce sprout from about 40°F.
    Warm crops
    Beans and corn at 60°F; tomatoes, peppers and squash at 65 to 70°F.
    Measure
    A soil thermometer 4 inches deep, in the morning, several days in a row.

    It happens every year. A sunny, 75-degree weekend arrives in April, garden centers fill with tomato and pepper starts, and everyone plants. Then the weather turns cool and wet again, and those plants sit there for a month, yellowing, or the bean seeds simply rot.

    The reason is that, west of the Cascades, the soil warms up weeks after the air does. Portland's typical last frost is March 13, but the soil a few inches down often stays cold into May. Frost dates tell you when it's safe from freezing. Soil temperature tells you when warm-season crops will actually grow.

    Soil temperatures to wait for

    • 40°F and up: peas, spinach, lettuce, radishes and other cool-season crops will sprout, slowly.
    • 50°F and up: carrots, beets, chard and onions grow well.
    • 60°F and up: bush beans, sweet corn and cucumbers. Bean seeds planted into colder, wet soil often rot before they sprout.
    • 65 to 70°F: tomatoes, peppers, squash and basil.

    How much difference a few degrees makes

    The numbers above aren't on-off switches. Seeds sprout faster and faster as soil warms, and the change is dramatic. In classic germination studies by the horticulturist J.F. Harrington, still reprinted in university vegetable guides, tomato seed took around six weeks to come up in 50°F soil, about two weeks at 59°F, and about a week at 68°F. Sweet corn took about three weeks at 50°F and under a week at 68°F. Lettuce, a cool-season crop, came up in about a week at 50°F and in just a few days at 60°F.

    A seed sitting in cold, wet soil for three weeks has three weeks for fungi, seedcorn maggots and slugs to find it. A row of beans sown into April soil often comes up patchy or not at all, and why the same seed sown two weeks later comes up in a few days.

    Why wet soil stays cold

    Water takes much more energy to warm than dry soil or air does. A bed that's soaked from weeks of spring rain uses up the sun's heat warming the water in it, so it lags behind a well-drained bed next to it. This is the real reason raised beds and sandy soils are ready earlier in the Northwest, and heavy clay that holds water is ready later. Anything that helps a bed drain, from raised beds to adding compost year after year, also helps it warm.

    How to measure it

    Push a soil thermometer about 4 inches into the bed in the morning, when soil is coolest, and check it for several days in a row. For small seeds sown shallowly, a reading at 2 inches is fine too. Once it holds at the target for about a week, you're ready. Soil thermometers cost around $10 at garden centers, and it's the cheapest garden tool that pays for itself. A simple dial probe or a cheap digital cooking thermometer both work; just leave it in for a minute before reading.

    Take readings in more than one bed if you can. You'll probably find that a bed against a south-facing wall, or a raised bed in full sun, is ready a week or two before a shady ground-level bed, and you can plan around that: tomatoes in the warm bed, a later sowing of beans in the cool one.

    How to warm soil faster

    • Raised beds drain and warm sooner than ground-level beds.
    • Black plastic laid over the bed for a week or two before planting raises the temperature several degrees. Cut holes and plant through it.
    • Clear plastic or a cold frame warms soil even faster for early sowings.
    • Wait to mulch. Straw and leaf mulches keep soil cool, which is great in July and a problem in May. Add them after the soil warms.

    What happens if you plant too early

    Cold soil doesn't just slow warm-season crops down. Bean, corn and squash seeds absorb water but can't grow, so they rot. Tomato and pepper transplants stop growing, and their leaves can turn purplish underneath because cold roots struggle to take up phosphorus. Plants set back this way often catch up only weeks after ones planted later into warm soil, so planting early rarely gains anything.

    Too warm matters too

    Soil temperature cuts both ways. Lettuce and spinach seed germinate poorly once soil goes much above about 80°F, which is why late-summer sowings for fall can come up patchy. Sow them in the cool of the evening, water the row well, and shade it with a board or a bit of shade cloth until the seedlings appear.

    A simple spring routine

    1. From March on, take a morning reading once or twice a week in your main beds and write it down.
    2. Sow cool-season crops once soil holds near 40°F and the soil is workable (it crumbles rather than staying in a sticky ball).
    3. Two weeks before you plan to plant warm crops, lay black plastic or set up a cloche or tunnel over that bed.
    4. Plant beans and corn when mornings hold at 60°F, and tomatoes, squash and peppers at 65°F or a little above, with cover ready for cold nights.
    5. Once the soil is warm, mulch to keep moisture in for summer.

    East of the Cascades

    In the drier inland valleys, soil warms faster in spring than on the west side, so the gap between frost dates and planting dates is shorter. But frost comes later and nights stay cold, so a warm afternoon reading can mislead. Check in the morning there, too, and keep frost cover handy after planting.
