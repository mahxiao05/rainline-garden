    What Your Last Frost Date Really Means

    A 'last frost date' is an average, and in half of years frost comes later. What the number means and how much margin to give tomatoes.

    By Jigmey Dorjee
    Posted October 2026 · Updated November 2026

    The short answer

    What it means
    The usual last frost date is a median: in half of years the last freeze comes later.
    Hardy crops
    Peas, spinach and kale can go in around the median date or even before it.
    Tender crops
    Wait for the 10% date and warm soil, with covers handy.

    Almost every planting chart is built around one number: your last frost date. Seed packets say "sow 2 weeks after last frost" and "start indoors 6 weeks before last frost." But that date is easy to misread, and the misreading is how a lot of tomato plants die in May.

    Maybe you've been there. You looked up your frost date, waited until it had safely passed, planted your tomatoes and basil on a warm weekend, and woke up a week later to blackened leaves. You didn't do anything wrong, exactly. You just trusted a number that was never meant to be a guarantee.

    It's an average, not a deadline

    The frost dates on Rainline Garden come from NOAA's U.S. Climate Normals, which summarize 30 years of daily temperature records (1991 to 2020) at official weather stations. For each station, NOAA works out the odds of a freeze, meaning 32°F or colder, after each day in spring.

    The date most charts call the "last frost date" is the median. In half of years the last spring freeze comes before it, and in half of years it comes after. Planting tender crops on that exact day is a coin flip.

    So Rainline Garden also shows a safer date: the day after which there's only a 10% chance of another freeze. Put simply, if you plant on that date, you'd expect to get caught by a frost about one spring in ten. At Portland International Airport, for example:

    • Median last freeze: March 13
    • Only a 10% chance of a freeze after: April 1

    Nearby towns can be weeks apart

    Frost dates change quickly with elevation and terrain. Cold air drains downhill on calm, clear nights and pools in low spots, so a valley floor can freeze when a hillside a mile away doesn't. Compare the median last freeze at a few stations:

    • Seattle-Tacoma airport: March 2
    • Portland airport: March 13
    • Olympia airport: April 29, nearly two months after Seattle even though the two are less than 60 miles apart
    • Redmond, for the Bend area: June 9

    Your own yard may run earlier or later than the nearest station. A low spot at the bottom of a slope tends to be colder; a bed against a south-facing wall in the city tends to be warmer.

    The Olympia number surprises a lot of people. The airport sits on an open, low prairie south of town, away from the warming effect of Puget Sound, and on clear spring nights cold air settles there. Seattle's airport is closer to the water and surrounded by a big city, both of which keep nights milder. Many towns have a story like this, which is why we show you the station your dates come from.

    Find out how your yard compares

    A minimum-maximum thermometer costs about the same as a bag of potting soil, and it's the best way to learn your own garden. Put it in the vegetable bed at about plant height, out of direct sun, and check it each morning through spring. After a few weeks, compare your lows to the forecast and the nearest station. If your yard runs a few degrees colder, push your planting dates later. If it runs warmer, you've earned a head start. Write it down, and next spring you won't have to guess.

    Frost can form above 32°F

    Official air temperatures are measured about five feet above the ground. On clear, still nights the ground and the leaves of low plants lose heat fast and can be several degrees colder than that. So a forecast low of 35°F can still leave frost on your lettuce. When the forecast is in the mid-30s, cover tender plants anyway.

    Not all freezes are equal

    Weather forecasters sometimes talk about a light freeze, a moderate freeze and a hard (or severe) freeze. Roughly, a light freeze is 29 to 32°F, which kills tender plants like basil, beans and tomatoes but barely bothers peas or kale. A moderate freeze, around 25 to 28°F, damages many leafy crops and fruit blossoms. A hard freeze, 24°F and below, does serious damage to most plants that aren't fully hardy. When you see a frost advisory or freeze warning, the number in the forecast tells you what to protect.

    Don't forget the fall date

    The first fall frost works the same way, in reverse. The typical date is a 50/50 point, so in half of years frost arrives earlier. At Portland airport the typical first fall freeze is around November 19; in Bend it's around September 14. That's the date to count back from when you plan fall crops, and the date to keep in mind when your tomatoes are still green in September. Frost often comes on the first clear, calm night after a cold front, so watch for those.

    Frost-free doesn't mean warm enough

    The last frost date only tells you when freezing becomes unlikely. Tomatoes, peppers, basil, beans and squash also need warm soil and mild nights, which in the Northwest often arrive weeks later. Rainline Garden's planting windows for warm crops start a while after the frost date for this reason. Read more in why to plant by soil temperature.

    How to use your dates

    1. Plant frost-hardy crops like peas, spinach and kale around the median date or even before it.
    2. Plant tender crops after the 10% date, and once the soil has warmed.
    3. Keep row cover or old sheets handy for a few weeks after you plant, in case of a late cold night.
    4. Watch the forecast, not just the calendar. A chart is a starting point; the weather this week is what matters.
