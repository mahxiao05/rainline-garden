    When to plant sweet corn

    Plant in the garden: Portland and Seattle May 10 – Jun 14; Spokane May 15 – Jun 19; Boise May 1 – Jun 5; Bend Jun 16 – Jul 21.

    Your dates in

      Sweet corn is corn picked young, while the kernels are still milky, and eaten fresh off the cob. Seed packets label varieties by sweetness, from standard (su) to sugary enhanced (se) and supersweet (sh2), and supersweets need to be kept apart from other types to stay sweet. Ears are at their sweetest right after picking, because the sugar starts turning to starch soon after harvest.

      How to grow sweet corn

      1. Prepare the bed: loosen the soil and mix in an inch or two of compost.
      2. Plant seeds on your town's dates, 1 inch deep, 10 inches apart in a block of short rows.
      3. Keep the soil evenly moist until sprouts appear, which can take one to three weeks in cool soil.
      4. Harvest: Ready about three weeks after the silks appear, when the silks are brown and the kernels squirt milky juice.

      Growing sweet corn in your part of the Northwest

      The same plant behaves differently on the wet west side, the foggy coast and the dry, colder east side.

      West-side valleys

      Willamette Valley, Puget Sound, southern Oregon

      Sweet corn needs warm soil to sprout, so wait until late May. Pick early to mid-season varieties; very late ones may not ripen in a cool summer.

      Coast

      Oregon and Washington coast

      Cool summers make sweet corn hard to ripen. Choose the earliest varieties and plant in your warmest spot.

      East of the Cascades

      Columbia Basin, Yakima, Boise, Spokane, north Idaho

      Hot summers suit corn well, and it is one of the classic east-side garden crops. Water deeply when tassels and silks appear, since drought then gives poorly filled ears.

      High desert and mountain towns

      Central Oregon, high eastern Oregon valleys, McCall, Salmon

      Frost risk and cool nights shorten the season, so plant the earliest varieties after the last frost. Black plastic warms the soil and speeds things up.

      How much to grow

      How many to plant

      Plant at least four rows side by side, so wind pollinates the ears. For a family of four, a block of about 50 to 75 plants.

      What to expect

      One or two good ears per plant.

      Storing the harvest

      Sweetness fades within hours of picking, so cook right away or blanch and freeze. Supersweet types hold a few days in the fridge.

      Growing notes

      What makes the difference in Northwest gardens, in plain language.

      Soil, warmth and feeding

      Corn is a warm-season grass that sprouts poorly in soil below about 60°F, and seed often rots in cold, wet ground. West of the Cascades that usually means waiting until mid to late May, while hot eastern valleys can plant a bit earlier. Supersweet types are even fussier about cold soil. Corn is a heavy nitrogen feeder: work in compost and fertilizer before planting, then side-dress when plants are about knee high. A pH of roughly 5.8 to 7.0 is fine.

      Pollination and why ears are half empty

      Each kernel forms only if a grain of pollen lands on its own silk strand. Pollen falls from the tassels on top and drifts on the wind, which is why corn is planted in a block of short rows instead of one long row. Gappy ears usually mean poor pollination from too few plants, hot dry weather at silking, or drought. Water deeply when tassels and silks appear. Also keep sweet corn types apart: if a supersweet crosses with a regular sweet corn, the kernels can turn starchy.

      Harvest, storage, and east vs west

      Sweet corn's sugar starts turning to starch the moment you pick it, so harvest right before cooking or chill ears quickly in the fridge. Check ears about three weeks after silking: brown silks, a full rounded tip and milky juice in the kernels mean ready. West of the Cascades, choose quicker varieties like Early Sunglow and Golden Bantam that finish before cool fall weather. East of the Cascades, longer-season types like Bodacious ripen easily, but watch for raccoons as ears near ripeness.

      Good neighbors and crop rotation

      Good neighbors

      • Pole or runner beans. They climb the stalks once the corn is a foot tall.
      • Winter squash. Shades the ground below and keeps weeds down.

      Grass family: Corn is a heavy nitrogen feeder with no close relatives in the vegetable garden. Follow it with peas or beans, or a cover crop, rather than another hungry crop.

      Pests and problems

      What most often goes wrong with sweet corn in Northwest gardens, how to spot it and what to do.

      Crows and jays

      Signs: Seedlings pulled up just after they sprout.

      What to do: Row cover or netting until plants are about 4 inches tall.

      Corn earworm

      Signs: A caterpillar eating the tip of the ear.

      What to do: Cut off the damaged tip; the rest is fine. A few drops of mineral oil on the silks after pollination deters them.

      Poorly filled ears

      Signs: Ears with gaps and missing kernels.

      What to do: Plant in blocks, not one long row, so pollen reaches every silk.

      Common questions about growing sweet corn

      When should I plant sweet corn in Oregon, Washington and Idaho?

      Plant in the garden: May 10 – Jun 14 in Portland and Seattle, May 15 – Jun 19 in Spokane, May 1 – Jun 5 in Boise and Jun 16 – Jul 21 in Bend. Pick your town at the top of the page for your own dates.

      Why are there missing kernels on my corn?

      Those kernels weren't pollinated. Plant in blocks of at least four short rows so pollen falls onto neighboring silks, and water well when plants tassel. Heat and drought at silking also cause gaps.

      When should I plant corn in western Oregon or Washington?

      Wait until the soil is at least 60°F, which is usually mid to late May. Seed planted earlier in cold, wet soil often rots before it sprouts. Black plastic or a clear cover for a week can warm the bed faster.

      Why isn't my sweet corn sweet?

      It may have been picked too late, stored too long, or crossed with another type of corn growing nearby. Pick at the milky stage and eat or chill it right away. Keep different sweet corn types apart or plant them to tassel at different times.
