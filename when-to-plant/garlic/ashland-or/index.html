    When to plant garlic in Ashland and Talent, OR

    Plant garlic in Ashland and Talent from Oct 1 – Nov 15. It grows slowly over winter and is ready to dig the next summer.

    Why these dates

    Garlic needs a cold winter to form bulbs, so it goes in during fall, after the soil cools but before it freezes hard. In Ashland and Talent the first fall frost usually comes around October 8.

    West-side valleys. The Willamette Valley, Puget Sound and southern Oregon valleys. Long frost-free seasons, but soil stays cold and wet into May, so warm crops wait for warm soil, not just the last frost.

    How to grow garlic in Ashland and Talent

    1. Buy seed garlic from a garden center or seed company. Grocery-store garlic is often a variety that doesn't suit Northwest winters.
    2. Loosen the soil and mix in an inch or two of compost.
    3. Break bulbs into cloves and plant them Oct 1 – Nov 15, 2 inches deep, pointed end up, 6 inches apart.
    4. Cover the bed with a few inches of straw or leaves for winter. Pull weeds in spring and stop watering in early summer when the leaves start to brown.
    5. Harvest: Dig in early summer when the lower third of the leaves are brown. Cure in a shady, airy spot for three weeks.

    Varieties that do well here

    • Music (hardneck)
    • Inchelium Red (softneck, from Washington)
    • Chesnok Red

    Watch out for

    Rust (orange spots on leaves) in wet springs. It's mostly cosmetic; give plants space and pull weeds.

    How it works

    The plant biology behind the advice above, in plain language.

    Why garlic is planted in fall

    A garlic clove needs a long spell of cold before it will divide into a new bulb with cloves. This is called vernalization, and it takes roughly six to eight weeks of temperatures below about 40°F, with hardneck types needing the most. Northwest winters provide that easily. Garlic planted in spring, or kept somewhere warm, often grows into a single round bulb with no cloves at all.

    Fall planting also gives cloves time to grow roots before the ground gets cold. Aim for a few weeks of rooting before a hard freeze; the green shoots may poke up in fall, and that's fine.

    Leaves now, bulb later

    Bulb size is set mostly by how much leaf the plant grows before it starts bulbing. Lengthening days and warming weather in late spring switch the plant from making leaves to filling the bulb, and each leaf becomes one of the papery wrappers around it. Anything that grows bigger, healthier leaves by early June, like good soil, steady moisture and spring feeding, means bigger heads.

    Feed with a nitrogen fertilizer when growth picks up in early spring and again a few weeks later, then stop by late May. Late nitrogen keeps the plant leafy and delays bulbing.

    Soil and water

    Garlic wants loose, well-drained soil with a pH of about 6.0 to 7.0 and plenty of compost. Cloves sitting in waterlogged winter soil rot, so raised beds help on heavy clay. Keep the soil moist in dry springs, roughly an inch of water a week, and stop watering about two weeks before harvest so the wrappers dry and tighten.

    Scapes

    Hardneck garlic sends up a curly flower stalk, the scape, in late spring. Snapping it off once it curls redirects energy into the bulb and generally gives noticeably bigger heads. Scapes are also good to eat, with a mild garlic flavor.

    Diseases worth knowing

    Rust shows up as orange spots on leaves in wet springs and is mostly cosmetic. The serious one is white rot, a soil fungus that turns roots and bulbs to white, fluffy mush. Its resting bodies survive in soil for 20 years or more, and once a bed has it, garlic and onions can't be grown there. It arrives on infected cloves or in soil carried on tools and boots, so start with clean seed garlic from a reputable grower, and never plant cloves from a bulb that looked soft or moldy.

    Harvest, curing and storage

    Dig when the lowest three or four leaves have turned brown but five or six are still green, usually late June to mid-July. Each green leaf is a wrapper still protecting the bulb. Wait too long and the wrappers break down and the cloves start to separate, which shortens storage.

    Cure whole plants in a shady, airy spot for two to four weeks until the necks are dry and tight, then trim. Store at cool room temperature with moderate humidity. Avoid the fridge: temperatures around 40 to 50°F tell cloves that winter is over and trigger sprouting. Softnecks usually keep six to nine months, hardnecks three to six.

    Common questions

    When is the last frost in Ashland and Talent, OR?

    Typically May 6. In 9 years out of 10, frost is over by May 27. These are 1991–2020 NOAA climate normals for Ashland.

    When is garlic ready to harvest in Ashland and Talent?

    Usually in early summer, about eight months after fall planting. Dig in early summer when the lower third of the leaves are brown. Cure in a shady, airy spot for three weeks.

    Can I plant garlic from the grocery store?

    It may grow, but it's a gamble. Store garlic is usually a softneck variety grown for a mild climate, may be treated to slow sprouting, and can carry diseases like white rot into your soil, where they last for decades. Seed garlic from a garden center or grower is a better bet.

    Why didn't my garlic form cloves?

    A single round bulb usually means the clove didn't get enough cold, which happens when garlic is planted in spring. It can also happen with very small cloves or crowded plants. Replant the round in fall and it will usually divide the following year.

    Should I cut garlic scapes?

    Yes, if you're growing hardneck garlic for bulbs. Snap them off once they curl; removing them generally gives noticeably bigger heads. Leave them on only if you plan to store the garlic a long time, since some growers find it keeps slightly better.

    When is garlic ready to dig?

    When the lowest three or four leaves are brown and five or six are still green, usually late June to mid-July in the Northwest. Dig a test bulb first: the cloves should fill the wrappers but still be tightly covered.
