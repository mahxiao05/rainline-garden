    How to grow mulberry trees

    1. Pick a sunny spot with soil that drains well, and mix in an inch or two of compost.
    2. Plant mulberry trees on your city's dates, 15 to 30 feet apart, depending on variety. Same depth as in the pot or nursery.
    3. Water deeply through the first two summers. Prune in late winter to keep the tree a size you can pick and net.
    4. Harvest: Ripe berries drop at a touch. Spread a sheet under the branches and shake gently. The harvest lasts several weeks in summer.

    Growing notes

    What makes the difference in Northwest gardens, in plain language.

    Choosing a mulberry

    Fruiting mulberries are fast, tough trees with sweet berries that look like long blackberries. Illinois Everbearing, a hybrid, is the most popular choice for flavor, hardiness and a long picking season.

    Full-size trees reach 30 feet or more. Gerardi Dwarf stays small enough for a large pot or small yard. Pakistan has very long fruit but is less cold-hardy, so it suits only the mildest gardens.

    Siting it right

    Ripe berries drop and stain. Plant over lawn or mulch, away from driveways, patios, sidewalks and where cars park. Birds love the fruit, and they spread the seeds.

    Mulberries grow in most soils in full sun and handle drought once established. Water deeply through the first two summers.

    Harvest and pruning

    Berries ripen over several weeks in summer. Spread a sheet under the tree and give the branches a gentle shake; ripe fruit falls. Eat them fresh or freeze them. They don't keep long.

    Prune in late winter to keep the tree a size you can pick and net. Mulberries bleed sap if pruned in spring, which is harmless but messy.

    Common questions about growing mulberry trees

    Do mulberries need a pollinator?

    Most fruiting varieties, including Illinois Everbearing, set fruit without a second tree.

    Will mulberries stain my patio?

    Yes. Dropped fruit stains paving and cars. Plant the tree over lawn or mulch.

    Which mulberry is best for the Northwest?

    Illinois Everbearing is the safest pick: hardy, productive and sweet. Gerardi Dwarf suits small spaces.
