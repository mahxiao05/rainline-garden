      How to grow kale

      1. Start seeds indoors on your city's dates in trays or small pots under a grow light or in your sunniest window. Or skip this step and buy young plants (starts) at a garden center.
      2. Harden off the young plants: about a week before planting, set them outside for a few hours a day, a little longer each day.
      3. Plant them in the garden on your city's dates, 18 inches apart. Plant starts at the depth they grew in the pot.
      4. Water deeply once or twice a week rather than a little every day, and mulch once the soil is warm.
      5. Plant again on your city's dates for a fall harvest.
      6. Harvest: Pick the lower leaves and let the top keep growing. Plants often produce from fall through spring west of the Cascades.

      More kale varieties

      More widely sold picks that do well somewhere in the Northwest. The tag says which side of the mountains suits each one best.

      Lacinato

      Dark blue-green, Italian · 60 days

      Also called dinosaur or Tuscan kale, with long, bumpy, dark leaves and a rich flavor. It is tender for kale and very good in soups and salads.

      Both sides

      Red Russian

      Flat-leaf, purple stems · 50 days

      Tender, oak-shaped gray-green leaves with purple stems and veins. Very cold hardy, it often produces through a mild Northwest winter and is great as baby greens.

      Winterbor

      Curly hybrid, cold hardy · 60 days

      Tall plants with thick, ruffled blue-green leaves. It is one of the hardiest curly kales and keeps producing through fall and much of winter.

      Dwarf Blue Curled Vates

      How much to grow

      How many to plant

      For a family of four, 4 to 6 plants keep you in kale for months. Plant a few more in late summer for winter picking.

      What to expect

      Each plant gives a handful of leaves a week for many months.

      Storing the harvest

      Leaves keep a week or more in a bag in the fridge. Blanch and freeze extras.

      The plant science

      The plant biology behind the advice above, in plain language.

      Why frost makes kale sweeter

      As temperatures drop, kale converts some of its starch into sugars, which act as a natural antifreeze in its cells. The result is noticeably sweeter, milder leaves after a few frosts. Hardy varieties like Winterbor and Red Russian survive into the low teens or below, and west of the Cascades kale often produces all winter.

      Soil and clubroot

      Kale grows best in fertile soil with a pH of about 6.0 to 7.5. In the Northwest, the main soil threat is clubroot, a soilborne disease of the whole cabbage family that causes swollen, distorted roots and wilting plants. It thrives in acidic, wet soil, and its spores survive in the ground for many years.

      If clubroot has shown up before, raise the pH to about 7.2 with lime at least a month before planting, improve drainage, and don't plant any cabbage-family crops in that spot for several years. Start with clean transplants and don't move soil from infected beds.

      Feeding and water

      Kale is a leaf crop, so it responds well to nitrogen. Mix compost and a balanced fertilizer into the bed, then side-dress with a little more nitrogen a month or so after planting. Keep the soil evenly moist; drought makes leaves tough and strong-flavored.

      Pests

      Cabbage aphids form gray, waxy colonies, often under leaves and in the growing tip. Blast them off with water, remove badly infested leaves, and let ladybugs and parasitic wasps do the rest. The green caterpillars are imported cabbageworms, the larvae of the white butterflies seen over gardens in summer. Row cover from planting day keeps the butterflies from laying eggs; Bt, a bacterial spray that affects only caterpillars, controls them if they're already there.

      Flowering in spring

      Kale is a biennial. After a winter of cold, it flowers the next spring. Before the flowers open, the tender shoots (often sold as kale raab) are sweet and good to eat. Once flowers open, pull the plant and compost it.

      Harvest and storage

      Pick the lowest, oldest leaves first and leave at least five or six leaves on top so the plant keeps growing. Kale stores best near freezing with high humidity, so keep it bagged in the fridge, where it lasts one to two weeks.

      Good neighbors and crop rotation

      Good neighbors

      • Lettuce and spinach. Fill the space under young kale before it grows large.
      • Onions and garlic. Narrow crops that tuck in around the big leaves.

      Keep apart

      • Other cabbage-family crops in the same bed every year. Shared pests and clubroot build up; rotate.

      Cabbage family: Grow cabbage-family crops in a different bed each year and come back to the same spot only every three or four years. Clubroot, which swells and knots the roots, lives in the soil for many years once it arrives, and root maggot pupae wait in last year's bed.

      Pests and problems

      What most often goes wrong with kale in Northwest gardens, how to spot it and what to do.

      Cabbage worms

      Signs: Green caterpillars and ragged holes; white moths fluttering over the bed.

      What to do: Insect netting from planting day, and pick off caterpillars.

      Gray aphids

      Signs: Waxy gray clusters under leaves and in the growing tip, worst in late summer.

      What to do: Spray off with water every few days and remove badly infested leaves.

      Cabbage root maggots

      Signs: Young transplants wilt and stall in spring.

      What to do: Row cover sealed at the edges, or a collar around each stem.

      Common questions about growing kale

      Does frost make kale sweeter?

      Yes. In cold weather kale turns some of its starches into sugars, which protect its cells from freezing. Leaves picked after a few frosts taste noticeably sweeter and milder.

      Why is my kale flowering in spring?

      Kale is a biennial, so after a winter of cold it goes to flower the next spring. Eat the tender flower shoots before they open, then pull the plant and start new kale for the year.

      How do I get rid of aphids on kale?

      Spray them off with a strong stream of water, remove badly infested leaves, and leave the ladybugs and tiny parasitic wasps that feed on them. Plants that are watered and fed well tolerate aphids better.
