      How to grow cilantro

      1. Prepare the bed: loosen the soil and mix in an inch or two of compost.
      2. Plant seeds on your city's dates, ¼ inch deep, 4 inches apart.
      3. Keep the soil evenly moist until sprouts appear, which can take one to three weeks in cool soil.
      4. Plant again on your city's dates for a fall harvest.
      5. Harvest: Cut leaves once plants are about 6 inches tall. If it bolts, let it go to seed and harvest the coriander.

      Growing notes

      What makes the difference in Northwest gardens, in plain language.

      Soil and sowing

      Cilantro grows best in fertile, well-drained soil with a pH of about 6.2 to 6.8 and steady moisture. It has a taproot and doesn't transplant well, so sow it directly where it will grow. Each round "seed" is actually a husk holding two seeds; gently cracking them before sowing can speed sprouting. Keep the soil damp until seedlings appear, usually within one to three weeks.

      Why cilantro bolts

      Cilantro is programmed to flower once days grow long and warm. Heat, dry soil and crowding all speed it up. Once a tall flower stalk rises, the leaves turn thin and feathery and the flavor changes. You can't stop it, but you can work around it: sow a short row every three weeks, give summer sowings afternoon shade, and grow slow-bolt varieties like Calypso, Santo and Leisure. In the Northwest, the most reliable crops come from early spring and late summer sowings. East of the Cascades, summer heat ends spring cilantro quickly, so early sowing matters even more.

      Harvest, coriander, and winter

      Cut leaves once plants are about 6 inches tall, taking the outer stems and leaving the center to regrow. Fresh cilantro keeps a week or so in the fridge with stems in a glass of water and a bag over the top. If plants bolt, let them flower: the blooms feed beneficial insects, and the seeds become coriander. Harvest when the seeds turn tan and dry them in a paper bag. West of the Cascades, a late summer sowing often survives mild winters and gives a spring harvest.

      Good neighbors and crop rotation

      Good neighbors

      • Lettuce. Same cool-season timing.
      • Squash and cabbage. Its flowers draw beneficial wasps and hoverflies.

      Carrot family: Carrots, parsnips, celery, parsley, dill and cilantro share pests. Carrot rust fly pupae overwinter in the soil where last year's carrots grew, so plant the next crop as far away as your garden allows and rotate on a three-year cycle.

      Pests and problems

      What most often goes wrong with cilantro in Northwest gardens, how to spot it and what to do.

      Bolting

      Signs: Plants flower and leaves turn feathery.

      What to do: Sow little and often in spring and fall; heat triggers bolting.

      Aphids

      Signs: Clusters on new growth.

      What to do: Hose them off.

      Common questions about growing cilantro

      Why does my cilantro keep going to seed?

      Cilantro naturally bolts as days lengthen and warm up. Sow small batches every few weeks, grow slow-bolt varieties, and give summer plantings some shade. Spring and fall crops last much longer than summer ones.

      Can I grow cilantro from coriander seeds?

      Yes. Coriander is cilantro seed, and whole seeds from the spice aisle often sprout. Seed packets sold for growing are more reliable and may be slow-bolting types.

      How do I harvest cilantro so it keeps growing?

      Cut the outer stems near the base and leave the young center stems in place. Take no more than about a third of the plant at once. It will keep producing until it starts to bolt.
