    Growing fruit and nut trees in the Northwest

    Fruit and nut trees are a long-term choice, so variety matters more here than for any other plant. The Northwest's wet springs make disease the main challenge: scab on apples, peach leaf curl on peaches, brown rot on cherries and plums, and eastern filbert blight on hazelnuts. Resistant varieties, listed on each tree's page, save years of spraying.

    Late winter to early spring is the time to plant bare-root trees, while they're dormant and the soil is moist. Choose a sunny spot with good air flow and soil that drains; trees sitting in winter-wet clay often decline. Many apples, pears, sweet cherries and plums need a second, compatible variety nearby for pollination, so check before you buy.

    Dwarf and semi-dwarf rootstocks keep trees small enough to prune, net and pick from the ground, which suits most home gardens. Expect two to five years before a real crop. Figs and self-fertile plums are among the easier trees for west-side gardens.

    Common questions

    When should I plant fruit trees in the Northwest?

    Bare-root trees go in from late winter to early spring while they're dormant. Potted trees can be planted in spring or fall.

    Which fruit trees are easiest west of the Cascades?

    Scab-resistant apples, Asian and European pears, figs in a warm spot, and self-fertile plums. Peaches are the hardest because of peach leaf curl in wet springs.

    Do I need two fruit trees for pollination?

    Many apples, pears, sweet cherries and plums need a second compatible variety nearby. Figs, quince, most persimmons, sour cherries and some plums fruit alone. Each tree's page says which.
