    How to grow peas

    1. Prepare the bed: loosen the soil and mix in an inch or two of compost.
    2. Plant seeds on your city's dates, 1 inch deep, 2 inches apart along a trellis.
    3. Keep the soil evenly moist until sprouts appear, which can take one to three weeks in cool soil.
    4. Harvest: Pick snap and snow peas when pods are plump but still crisp, every two or three days.

    Varieties that do well here

    • Oregon Sugar Pod II
    • Sugar Snap
    • Green Arrow

    Watch out for

    Birds and slugs go after young shoots. Cover rows with netting until plants are a few inches tall.

    Popular peas varieties

    Widely sold picks that do well somewhere in Oregon or Washington. The tag says which side of the mountains suits each one best.

    Oregon Sugar Pod II

    Snow pea, climbing · 68 days

    Flat, sweet snow peas on vines about 30 inches tall. It resists powdery mildew and pea enation virus, two common Northwest pea problems, and keeps producing well.

    Both sides

    Sugar Snap

    Snap pea, tall vine · 70 days

    The classic edible-pod snap pea with thick, juicy, very sweet pods. Vines reach 5 to 6 feet, so give it a sturdy trellis and sow early to beat summer heat.

    Both sides

    Green Arrow

    Shelling pea, bush · 68 days

    Long pods packed with 9 to 11 sweet peas, usually borne in pairs. It resists fusarium wilt and powdery mildew and needs only a short support.

    Both sides

    Cascadia

    Snap pea, short vine · 60 days

    A compact snap pea on vines about 30 inches tall with crisp, sweet pods. It resists powdery mildew and pea enation virus, which makes it a dependable choice west of the Cascades.

    Both sides

    Sugar Ann

    Snap pea, dwarf · 56 days

    One of the earliest snap peas, on short vines that need little or no support. Good for containers, small beds and places where summer heat or a short season cuts the pea window.

    How it works

    The plant biology behind the advice above, in plain language.

    A crop built for cool weather

    Peas sprout in soil as cool as 40°F, though they come up much faster as it warms toward 60°F, and they grow best when days are 60 to 70°F. Seedlings tolerate light frost. Production drops once temperatures regularly top about 80°F, which is why peas go in early, from February to April depending on where you live.

    The biggest early risk isn't cold but wet. Seeds sitting in cold, waterlogged soil rot, so plant into soil that crumbles in your hand rather than forming a muddy ball.

    Nitrogen from the air

    Like beans, peas host rhizobia bacteria in root nodules that turn nitrogen from the air into fertilizer for the plant. They need little added nitrogen, and an inoculant helps in beds that haven't grown peas recently.

    A common belief is that peas leave lots of nitrogen in the soil. In fact, most of it ends up in the seeds you harvest. Cutting the vines and leaving the roots in the ground adds organic matter, but only a modest amount of nitrogen.

    Soil and water

    Peas like well-drained soil with a pH of about 6.0 to 7.5. Keep the soil moist once flowering starts, since dry spells during bloom and pod fill cut yields. A trellis gives better airflow and easier picking, even for shorter varieties.

    Diseases

    Powdery mildew shows up as peas wind down in summer heat. Pea enation mosaic virus, spread by aphids, causes stunted, deformed plants and is a particular problem for later plantings in the Northwest. Early planting and resistant varieties like Oregon Sugar Pod II help with both.

    Why fresh peas taste sweeter

    Pea sugars start converting to starch as soon as the pods are picked, and the conversion is fastest when they're warm. That's why homegrown peas taste so much sweeter than store-bought ones. Pick in the cool morning, refrigerate right away, and eat or blanch and freeze them within a day or two.

    Pick snap and snow peas when pods are plump but crisp, and shelling peas when the peas fill the pod but the pod is still bright green. Frequent picking keeps plants producing.

    Common questions about growing peas

    How cold can peas handle?

    Peas sprout in soil as cool as 40°F and seedlings tolerate light frost. Hard freezes can damage flowers and pods, so cover plants if a cold snap is forecast once they're blooming.

    Do pea roots add nitrogen to the soil?

    A little. Most of the nitrogen peas fix goes into the seeds you harvest. Leaving the roots in the ground adds organic matter and a modest amount of nitrogen, so it's still worth doing.

    Why are my pea plants turning yellow from the bottom?

    Late in the season, it's natural aging as summer heat arrives. Earlier, yellowing can mean waterlogged soil, root rot, or a lack of nitrogen-fixing nodules. Check the roots for pink nodules and make sure the soil drains.
