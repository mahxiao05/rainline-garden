    When to plant zucchini and summer squash

    Plant in the garden: Portland and Seattle May 15 – Jun 12; Spokane May 20 – Jun 17; Boise May 1–29; Bend Jun 9 – Jul 7.

    Your dates in

      Zucchini and other summer squash are bushy squash-family plants picked young, while the skin is soft and the seeds tiny, for grilling, sautéing and baking into bread. Besides green zucchini there are yellow crookneck and straightneck squash and flying-saucer-shaped pattypans. The big yellow flowers are edible too, often stuffed and fried.

      How to grow zucchini and summer squash

      1. Start seeds indoors on your town's dates in trays or small pots under a grow light or in your sunniest window. Or skip this step and buy young plants (starts) at a garden center.
      2. Harden off the young plants: about a week before planting, set them outside for a few hours a day, a little longer each day.
      3. Plant them in the garden on your town's dates, 3 feet apart. If you plant seeds instead, sow them 1 inch deep.
      4. Water deeply once or twice a week rather than a little every day, and mulch once the soil is warm.
      5. Harvest: Pick zucchini at 6 to 8 inches. Miss a few days and you'll have a baseball bat.

      Growing zucchini and summer squash in your part of the Northwest

      The same plant behaves differently on the wet west side, the foggy coast and the dry, colder east side.

      West-side valleys

      Willamette Valley, Puget Sound, southern Oregon

      Zucchini grows well once nights warm in June. Powdery mildew coats leaves by late summer, and slugs chew young plants.

      Coast

      Oregon and Washington coast

      Summer squash is one of the easier warm crops on the coast, though plants grow more slowly and mildew comes early in damp weather.

      East of the Cascades

      Columbia Basin, Yakima, Boise, Spokane, north Idaho

      Summer squash thrives in the heat. Water deeply and watch for squash bugs, which are more common east of the Cascades.

      High desert and mountain towns

      Central Oregon, high eastern Oregon valleys, McCall, Salmon

      Plant after the last frost and cover young plants on cold nights. Zucchini usually still produces well in a short season.

      How much to grow

      How many to plant

      For a family of four, two or three plants is plenty. One plant gives a zucchini every day or two at peak.

      What to expect

      About 6 to 10 pounds per plant.

      Storing the harvest

      Keep a week in the fridge. Grate and freeze extras for bread and soups.

      The plant science

      The plant biology behind the advice above, in plain language.

      Warm soil first

      Squash seeds need soil of at least 60°F to sprout, and they come up fastest at 70 to 95°F. In cold, wet soil they rot before they emerge, which is why squash sown too early in the Northwest often fails. Plants are also damaged by long spells below about 50°F. Wait for warm soil, or start seeds indoors two to three weeks before planting out.

      Male and female flowers

      Squash plants make separate male and female flowers. Males usually appear first, often for a week or more, and those early blossoms fall off without producing anything. That's normal. Female flowers have a tiny squash at their base.

      Each flower opens for one morning only, and bees have to carry pollen from a male to a female. In cool or rainy weather, few bees fly. Poorly pollinated fruit starts to grow, then turns yellow and shrivels from the tip. If that's happening, hand-pollinate: early in the morning, pick a male flower, peel back its petals and brush its pollen onto the center of an open female flower.

      Soil, water and feeding

      Squash are heavy feeders. Grow them in soil rich in compost with a pH of about 6.0 to 7.5, and give them about an inch of water a week, at the base of the plant. Big leaves often wilt in afternoon heat even when the soil is moist; if they recover by evening, the plant is fine.

      Powdery mildew

      The white, powdery coating on older leaves in late summer is powdery mildew. Unlike most fungal diseases, it doesn't need wet leaves to spread; it thrives with warm days, cool nights and humid air, which is typical of a Northwest August. Plant mildew-resistant varieties, space plants for airflow and remove the worst leaves. Late in the season it's mostly a sign the plant is winding down.

      Harvest and storage

      Zucchini is eaten unripe, so pick it at 6 to 8 inches, when the skin is glossy and seeds are still tiny. Letting fruit grow huge signals the plant to slow down, so frequent picking keeps it producing. Store it in the fridge's crisper drawer and use within about a week; it gets pitted and soft if kept cold for too long.

      Good neighbors and crop rotation

      Good neighbors

      • Nasturtiums and borage. Draw bees that pollinate the flowers.
      • Corn. Squash leaves shade the soil under the stalks.

      Squash family: Move squash, pumpkins, cucumbers and melons to a new bed each year and return every three years, so soil-borne wilts and root rots don't build up. Clear and compost (or bin) mildewed vines in fall.

      Pests and problems

      What most often goes wrong with zucchini and summer squash in Northwest gardens, how to spot it and what to do.

      Powdery mildew

      Signs: White powder on the leaves from August.

      What to do: Remove the worst leaves, water at the base and grow resistant varieties.

      Slugs

      Signs: Young plants chewed off in damp spring weather.

      What to do: Start plants indoors and set them out with slug bait around them.

      Fruit rots at the tip

      Signs: Small squash shrivel and rot from the flower end.

      What to do: Often poor pollination in cool weather. Hand-pollinate with a paintbrush and water evenly.

      Common questions about growing zucchini and summer squash

      When should I plant zucchini and summer squash in Oregon, Washington and Idaho?

      Plant in the garden: May 15 – Jun 12 in Portland and Seattle, May 20 – Jun 17 in Spokane, May 1–29 in Boise and Jun 9 – Jul 7 in Bend. Pick your town at the top of the page for your own dates.

      Why are my zucchini rotting at the tip?

      Usually poor pollination. When fruit isn't fully pollinated it starts to grow, then yellows and rots from the blossom end. Cool, rainy weather keeps bees away. Hand-pollinate in the morning with a male flower, or wait for warmer weather.

      Why does my squash have flowers but no fruit?

      Squash produce male flowers first, often for a week or more, and those don't make fruit. Female flowers, with a tiny squash at the base, follow. If both are present and fruit still doesn't set, there may not be enough bees, and hand-pollinating helps.

      What is the white powder on my squash leaves?

      Powdery mildew, which is common in late summer. Remove the worst leaves, keep watering at the base, and choose resistant varieties next year. Plants usually keep producing for weeks.
