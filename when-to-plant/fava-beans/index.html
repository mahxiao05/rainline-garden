    How to grow fava beans

    1. Prepare the bed: loosen the soil and mix in an inch or two of compost.
    2. Plant seeds on your city's dates, 1 to 2 inches deep, 6 inches apart.
    3. Keep the soil evenly moist until sprouts appear, which can take one to three weeks in cool soil.
    4. Plant again on your city's dates for a fall harvest.
    5. Harvest: Pick pods when they bulge with beans but are still green. Shell the beans and slip off the pale skins for the sweetest flavor.

    Varieties that do well here

    • Broad Windsor
    • Aquadulce Claudia (for fall sowing)
    • Vroma

    Watch out for

    Black aphids gather on the growing tips in late spring. Pinch off the tips once pods start forming.

    Large-seeded, spring · 85 days

    The classic fava with large, flat beans, about three to five per pod. Sow as early in spring as the soil can be worked; it thrives in cool weather.

    Aquadulce Claudia

    Overwintering, large-seeded · Sow fall, pick late spring

    An old Spanish variety known for winter hardiness. Sown in fall west of the Cascades, it gives one of the earliest harvests of the year.

    West side

    Vroma

    Large-seeded, upright

    Upright plants with long pods packed with large green beans. A reliable producer from early spring sowings. Sow it as early in spring as the soil can be worked, since favas like cool weather.

    Both sides

    Sweet Lorane

    Small-seeded, very hardy · Sow fall or spring

    Named for Lorane, Oregon, with small, sweet beans. Very cold hardy, it is popular as an overwintering cover crop that also yields good dry beans.

    Growing notes

    What makes the difference in Northwest gardens, in plain language.

    A bean that likes cool weather

    Unlike common beans, favas sprout in cool soil and grow best in mild weather, which suits Northwest springs perfectly. Sow as soon as the soil can be worked in late winter or early spring. Hot weather makes them stop flowering, so early sowing is the key to a good crop.

    West of the Cascades, you can also sow hardy varieties like Aquadulce Claudia in October. They grow a little, sit through winter and take off early, giving the earliest harvest. In cold eastern areas, stick to spring sowing.

    Soil and feeding

    Favas aren't fussy. Any reasonably drained soil works, and like other beans they fix their own nitrogen, so skip the fertilizer. They're also grown as a cover crop for this reason: chopping the plants into the soil before they set seed adds organic matter and nitrogen.

    Tall varieties can flop in wind and heavy rain. A string run along each side of the row, tied to stakes, keeps them upright.

    Aphids and chocolate spot

    Black bean aphids crowd onto the soft growing tips in late spring. Pinching off the top few inches once the lowest pods form removes most of them and pushes the plant's energy into the pods. The tips are tasty cooked like spinach.

    Chocolate spot is a fungus that causes small brown spots on leaves during long wet spells. It's rarely serious on well-spaced plants in sun. Give rows room for airflow and don't crowd fall plantings.

    Common questions about growing fava beans

    Can you plant fava beans in the fall?

    Yes, west of the Cascades. Sow hardy types like Aquadulce Claudia in October for an early harvest the next spring. Very cold snaps can still damage them, so east of the mountains spring sowing is safer.

    Do I have to peel fava beans?

    Young, small beans can be eaten with their skins. Larger beans have a tough pale skin, so blanch them for a minute, cool them, and pinch the bright green bean out. It's extra work but makes them much sweeter.

    Are fava beans safe for everyone?

    Most people eat them without trouble. People with an inherited condition called G6PD deficiency can have a serious reaction to eating favas, and sometimes even to the pollen. If you or your family have that condition, skip this crop.
