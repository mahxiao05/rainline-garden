    How to grow dill

    1. Prepare the bed: loosen the soil and mix in an inch or two of compost.
    2. Plant seeds on your city's dates, ¼ inch deep, 12 inches apart.
    3. Keep the soil evenly moist until sprouts appear, which can take one to three weeks in cool soil.
    4. Harvest: Snip leaves any time. For pickling, cut the flower heads just as they open.

    Watch out for

    Tall plants flop in wind and rain. Grow 'Fernleaf', a compact variety, or give it a stake.

    More dill varieties

    More widely sold picks that do well somewhere in the Northwest. The tag says which side of the mountains suits each one best.

    Fernleaf

    Compact, slow to bolt

    A dwarf dill about 18 inches tall that stays upright in wind and rain. It is slow to bolt and fits well in containers and small beds.

    Both sides

    Bouquet

    Standard, early flowering

    A popular all-purpose dill with plenty of leaves and large seed heads. It flowers fairly early, making it a good choice for pickling and seed.

    Both sides

    Mammoth

    Tall, large heads

    Also sold as Long Island Mammoth, it grows tall with big flower and seed heads for pickling. Give it a stake or a sheltered spot so it doesn't flop.

    Both sides

    Dukat

    Leafy, slow to bolt

    Grown mainly for its abundant, sweet-flavored foliage, with a bright green color. It is slow to go to seed, so you can harvest leaves for longer.

    Both sides

    Hera

    Slow-bolt leaf dill

    A leafy dill selected to delay flowering, giving a longer harvest of fresh leaves. Useful for repeated sowings through spring and early summer.

    For a family of four, a 3-foot row, plus a few plants near the cucumbers for pickling.

    Freeze leaves; dried dill loses flavor fast. Dry the seed heads for pickling.

    Growing notes

    What makes the difference in Northwest gardens, in plain language.

    Soil and sowing

    Dill grows in average, well-drained soil with a pH around 5.8 to 6.8 and doesn't need much fertilizer. Rich soil makes soft, floppy plants. It has a long taproot and sulks after transplanting, so sow seed where it will grow, about a quarter inch deep, after the soil has warmed a bit. Keep the soil moist until seedlings appear. Sow a few more seeds every three or four weeks through early summer for a steady supply of leaves.

    Why dill bolts and how to time it

    Dill naturally heads for flowering as summer days get long and warm, and after that it makes few new leaves. If you grow it mainly for leaves, choose Fernleaf or Bouquet and sow several times. If you want flower heads for pickles, time one sowing about two months before cucumbers start producing, since the two are harvested together. Dill and fennel can cross, which spoils the flavor of saved seed, so keep them apart. West of the Cascades, stake taller types or grow compact Fernleaf to handle rain and wind.

    Insects, harvest and seed

    Dill flowers are excellent for beneficial insects like ladybugs, lacewings and tiny parasitic wasps that help control aphids on nearby crops. Aphids sometimes cluster on dill itself; a spray of water clears them. Snip leaves any time. They wilt fast, so use them quickly or freeze them. For seed, let flower heads turn brown, then cut them into a paper bag to dry. Dill self-sows readily, so expect volunteer seedlings next spring.

    Good neighbors and crop rotation

    Good neighbors

    • Cucumbers. Ready at the same time for pickling.
    • Cabbage-family crops. Its flowers draw wasps and hoverflies that prey on caterpillars and aphids.

    Keep apart

    • Carrots. Dill can cross with carrots if both flower, and shares carrot rust fly.

    Carrot family: Carrots, parsnips, celery, parsley, dill and cilantro share pests. Carrot rust fly pupae overwinter in the soil where last year's carrots grew, so plant the next crop as far away as your garden allows and rotate on a three-year cycle.

    Pests and problems

    Signs: Clusters on the stems.

    What to do: Hose them off.

    Flopping

    Common questions about growing dill

    Why is my dill flowering so soon?

    Long, warm days push dill to flower. Sow small batches every few weeks and choose leafy types like Fernleaf. Letting some plants flower is fine; they feed beneficial insects.

    Can I transplant dill?

    Dill has a taproot and usually stalls or bolts after being moved. Sow it directly in the garden instead. If you do buy starts, plant them young and disturb the roots as little as possible.

    When do I harvest dill for pickles?

    Cut the flower heads just as they open, when they are still bright yellow. Plan a sowing about two months before your cucumbers are ready so both crops line up.
