      How to grow pear trees

      1. Pick a sunny spot with soil that drains well, and mix in an inch or two of compost.
      2. Plant pear trees on your city's dates, 12 to 15 feet apart for dwarf trees, 18 to 20 feet for standard. Graft union 2 to 3 inches above the soil.
      3. Water deeply through the first two summers. Pears need little pruning; take out crossing branches in late winter and keep the center open.
      4. Harvest: Pick European pears when they're full size and lift off easily but are still hard, then ripen them at room temperature. Asian pears ripen on the tree; pick when sweet and crisp.

      Top picks for here

      • Bartlett
      • Comice
      • Seckel (small, very sweet)
      • Shinseiki (Asian)

      Watch out for

      Pear scab in wet springs and fire blight in warm, humid bloom weather (more common east of the Cascades). Cut out blackened, shepherd's-crook shoots well below the damage.

      More pear varieties

      More widely sold picks that do well somewhere in the Northwest. The tag says which side of the mountains suits each one best.

      Bartlett

      European, best with partner · Ripens late August

      The classic juicy, buttery yellow pear for eating fresh and canning. Pick it full size but firm and ripen indoors; it sets better crops with another pear variety nearby.

      Both sides

      Comice

      European, needs partner · Ripens late September

      Considered the finest-flavored pear, with sweet, melting, aromatic flesh. It needs a pollinator such as Bartlett and benefits from a few weeks of cold storage before ripening.

      Both sides

      Seckel

      Small European dessert pear · Ripens September

      Small, russeted pears with spicy, intensely sweet flavor, ideal for snacking and preserving. The tree is compact and has good resistance to fire blight.

      Both sides

      Shinseiki

      Asian pear, partly self-fertile · Ripens August

      Round, crisp, juicy yellow fruit that ripens on the tree. It bears young and heavily, and it also pollinates other Asian and many European pears.

      Both sides

      Bosc

      European, russeted · Ripens September to October

      Long-necked brown pears with dense, sweet, spicy flesh that holds its shape when baked or poached. It needs another pear for pollination and stores well.

      Both sides

      D'Anjou

      European, long-storing · Ripens late September

      Mild, sweet and juicy, with fruit that keeps in cold storage for months. Plant it with another pear variety for good crops.

      Chojuro

      Asian pear, russeted · Ripens late August to September

      Golden-brown Asian pears with a rich, almost butterscotch flavor and crisp texture. It stores well for an Asian pear and needs another Asian or European pear for pollination.

      Growing notes

      What makes the difference in Northwest gardens, in plain language.

      Choosing varieties that pollinate each other

      Pears are some of the most forgiving fruit trees for west-side gardens: they tolerate heavy, damp soil better than most fruit trees and need little spraying. Most European pears need a second variety that blooms at the same time. Bartlett, Comice and Seckel can pollinate each other, though Bartlett and Seckel are a poor match. Asian pears like Shinseiki bloom early, but they often overlap enough with early European pears to help with pollination as well.

      Choose a rootstock suited to your space. Dwarfing pear rootstocks still make fairly large trees, so leave room as guide spacing suggests.

      Why European pears ripen off the tree

      A European pear left on the tree ripens from the inside out and turns gritty or mushy at the core. Pick them when they reach full size and the stem snaps off with a gentle lift, while the flesh is still firm. Bartlett ripens at room temperature in about a week. Comice and Anjou-type pears ripen better after a few weeks in the refrigerator, then a few days on the counter. Check ripeness by pressing gently near the stem: when it gives, it's ready.

      Pests and diseases in the Northwest

      Pear scab works like apple scab and spreads from fallen leaves in wet springs, so rake leaves each fall. Fire blight is a bacterial disease that thrives in warm, humid weather during bloom; it's more common east of the Cascades. Prune out blackened shoots in dry weather, cutting 8 to 12 inches below the damage and wiping your shears with disinfectant between cuts.

      West of the Cascades, pear trellis rust causes bright orange spots on leaves. It spends part of its life on junipers, so it's worse near juniper hedges, and it's mostly cosmetic.

      Good neighbors

      Good neighbors

      • Another pear variety. Cross-pollination.

      Keep apart

      • Junipers close by. They host pear trellis rust, which spots pear leaves orange.

      Pests and problems

      What most often goes wrong with pear trees in Northwest gardens, how to spot it and what to do.

      Pear scab

      Signs: Dark spots on leaves and cracked fruit.

      What to do: Rake up fallen leaves and choose resistant varieties.

      Fire blight

      Signs: Shoot tips blacken and curl like a shepherd's crook.

      What to do: Cut 8 to 12 inches below the damage on a dry day and clean pruners between cuts.

      Pear slug

      Signs: Slimy, dark larvae skeletonizing leaves in summer.

      What to do: Hose them off or dust leaves with fine soil or ash.

      Common questions about growing pear trees

      Why are my pears gritty or brown in the middle?

      They were left on the tree too long. European pears should be picked while still hard and ripened indoors. Pick when the fruit reaches full size and lifts off the branch easily.

      Do I need two pear trees?

      For most European pears, yes. Plant two varieties that bloom at the same time, such as Bartlett and Comice. A neighbor's pear tree nearby can also do the job.

      How long can you store homegrown pears?

      Bartlett keeps for a few weeks in the refrigerator, while Comice and other winter pears can keep for a couple of months. Bring them out a few days before you want to eat them so they can soften.
