      Red-flowering currant is a native deciduous shrub, usually 5 to 10 feet tall, grown mainly for its hanging clusters of pink to deep red flowers. Its small blue-black berries are edible but bland and mostly eaten by birds. Botanist David Douglas sent it to Britain in the 1820s, and it's still a common garden shrub there.

      How to grow red-flowering currant

      1. Pick a sunny spot with soil that drains well, and mix in an inch or two of compost.
      2. Plant red-flowering currant shrubs on your town's dates, 4 to 6 feet apart. Same depth as in the pot.
      3. Water through the first two summers, then occasionally in drought. Prune right after flowering, cutting a few old stems to the base.
      4. Harvest: Grown for its flowers rather than fruit. The small blue-black berries are edible but bland; birds love them.

      Growing notes

      What makes the difference in Northwest gardens, in plain language.

      Where it grows wild

      Red-flowering currant grows naturally in open woods, forest edges and clearings, mostly west of the Cascades from British Columbia into California. It's a deciduous shrub that usually reaches 6 to 10 feet tall and a bit narrower. The hanging clusters of pink to deep red flowers appear in late winter or early spring, often before the leaves are fully out, which makes it one of the first native shrubs to bloom.

      Planting and pruning

      Plant in full sun to part shade in soil that drains reasonably well. More sun means more flowers and less powdery mildew. West of the Cascades, fall planting is ideal because winter rain settles the roots before summer. Water through the first two summers, then only during long droughts.

      Prune right after flowering, since next year's blooms form on this year's growth. Cut a few of the oldest, woodiest stems to the base every year or two to keep the shrub young and full of flowers. King Edward VII stays a little more compact.

      Wildlife value

      The flowers open just as rufous hummingbirds return from the south in early spring, and they're one of the birds' most important early nectar sources. Native bees, including bumblebee queens, visit the flowers too. The small blue-black berries ripen in summer and get eaten by birds. Planting red-flowering currant is one of the easiest ways to bring hummingbirds into a Northwest garden.

      Good neighbors

      Grows well with

      • Serviceberry and Oregon grape. The same sun to part shade and well-drained soil.
      • Salmonberry. Blooms at the same time; together they feed hummingbirds returning in early spring.

      Pests and problems

      What most often goes wrong with red-flowering currant in Northwest gardens, how to spot it and what to do.

      Powdery mildew

      Signs: White coating on the leaves, worst in shade.

      What to do: Plant in sun with good airflow.

      Imported currantworm

      Signs: Green larvae strip the leaves in spring.

      What to do: Check under the lower leaves in April and May and pick them off.

      Summer leaf drop

      Signs: Leaves yellow and drop in a dry August.

      What to do: Normal in dry spots. Water deeply once or twice a month in summer for a tidier shrub.

      Common questions about growing red-flowering currant
      When should I plant red-flowering currant in Oregon, Washington and Idaho?

      Plant in the garden: Mar 1 – Apr 26 in Portland and Seattle, Apr 1 – May 27 in Spokane, Mar 20 – May 15 in Boise and Apr 28 – Jun 23 in Bend. Pick your town at the top of the page for your own dates.

      When does red-flowering currant bloom?

      From late winter into early spring, usually around March, depending on the year and location. It's one of the first native shrubs to flower.

      Are red-flowering currant berries edible?

      Yes, but they're small, seedy and bland. Most gardeners leave them for the birds.

      Does red-flowering currant attract hummingbirds?

      Yes, it's one of the best plants for them in the Northwest. It blooms right as rufous hummingbirds arrive in spring.
