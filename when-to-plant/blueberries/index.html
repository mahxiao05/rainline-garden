    When to plant blueberries

    Plant in the garden: Portland and Seattle Mar 15 – May 3; Spokane Apr 15 – Jun 3; Boise Apr 1 – May 20; Bend May 12 – Jun 30.

    Your dates in

      Blueberries are woody shrubs in the heath family, related to huckleberries, rhododendrons and heather, grown for berries eaten fresh, frozen or baked. Most garden bushes are northern highbush types, while half-high varieties stay small enough for big pots, and a healthy bush can keep producing for decades. Oregon and Washington are two of the country's biggest blueberry-growing states.

      How to grow blueberries

      1. Pick a sunny spot with soil that drains well, and mix in an inch or two of compost.
      2. Plant blueberry bushes on your town's dates, 4 to 5 feet apart. Same depth as in the pot.
      3. Mix peat moss or acidic compost into the planting hole, mulch with sawdust or bark, and pinch off flowers the first year.
      4. Harvest: Pick berries a few days after they turn fully blue, when they roll off into your hand.

      Growing blueberries in your part of the Northwest

      The same plant behaves differently on the wet west side, the foggy coast and the dry, colder east side.

      West-side valleys

      Willamette Valley, Puget Sound, southern Oregon

      West-side acidic soils suit blueberries naturally, and the region grows many commercially. Mummy berry and spotted wing drosophila are the main pests.

      Coast

      Oregon and Washington coast

      Blueberries grow well in the acidic coastal soils. Choose varieties that ripen before fall rains.

      East of the Cascades

      Columbia Basin, Yakima, Boise, Spokane, north Idaho

      Alkaline soil and water make blueberries hard east of the Cascades. Grow them in raised beds with acidic mix or large containers, and watch for yellow leaves, a sign of iron deficiency.

      High desert and mountain towns

      Central Oregon, high eastern Oregon valleys, McCall, Salmon

      Alkaline soil and cold winters make blueberries challenging. Use half-high varieties in containers or acidified beds.

      More blueberry varieties

      More widely sold picks that do well somewhere in the Northwest. The tag says which part of the Northwest suits each one best.

      Duke

      Northern highbush, early · Ripens early July

      One of the earliest and most dependable blueberries, with mild, firm, medium-large berries. It blooms late, so spring frosts rarely catch it, and it is a heavy, reliable cropper.

      Both sides

      Bluecrop

      Northern highbush, midseason · Ripens mid July

      The most widely planted blueberry for good reason: steady heavy crops of light blue, tangy-sweet berries on a hardy, upright bush. A safe first choice for almost any Northwest yard.

      Both sides

      Chandler

      Northern highbush, late · Ripens late July

      Famous for some of the largest berries of any blueberry, often the size of a nickel or bigger. It ripens over a long stretch, extending the harvest well into August.

      Both sides

      Top Hat

      Dwarf half-high · Ripens midsummer

      A compact bush about 2 feet tall that is made for patio pots. It is very cold hardy and bears small, flavorful berries, with white spring flowers and red fall leaves as a bonus.

      Cold winters

      Patriot

      Northern highbush, early · Ripens early July

      Very cold hardy and more tolerant of heavy, wet soil than most blueberries. Large, flavorful berries come early, and the bush puts on bright orange-red color in fall.

      Both sides

      Legacy

      Highbush, late midseason · Ripens late July

      Prized for its outstanding sweet flavor and high yields. The bush holds some leaves through mild winters, and it does best in the gentler winters west of the Cascades.

      West side

      Reka

      Northern highbush, early · Ripens early July

      A vigorous bush that tolerates wetter soils better than many varieties. Dark blue, medium berries with good flavor come early, and the plant grows quickly to full production.

      How much to grow

      How many to plant

      For a family of four, 4 to 8 bushes, with two or more varieties for better crops and a longer season.

      What to expect

      About 5 to 10 pounds per bush once mature, usually by year six to eight.

      Storing the harvest

      Keep unwashed in the fridge for one to two weeks. Freeze on a tray, then bag.

      The plant science

      The plant biology behind the advice above, in plain language.

      Acidic soil is the whole game

      Blueberries need acidic soil, with a pH of about 4.5 to 5.5. Many Northwest soils west of the Cascades are already close, but soils east of the mountains are often neutral or alkaline. Test before you plant. At the wrong pH, blueberries can't take up iron and other nutrients, and the classic symptom is yellow leaves with green veins.

      To lower pH, mix elemental sulfur into the soil. Soil bacteria slowly turn it into acid, which takes months, so ideally add it the season before planting and retest. Avoid aluminum sulfate, which acts faster but can build up to levels that harm the roots. If your soil is alkaline, growing in large containers or raised beds of acidic mix is often easier than fighting it.

      Shallow, fussy roots

      Blueberry roots are fine, shallow, and have no root hairs. Instead they rely on partnerships with soil fungi to absorb water and nutrients, and those fungi do best in soil rich in organic matter. That's why growers work peat or acidic compost into the planting area and keep 2 to 4 inches of sawdust or bark mulch over the roots. The mulch keeps them cool and moist and slowly adds organic matter.

      Shallow roots also dry out fast. Give them about an inch or two of water a week in summer, especially while berries are swelling, ideally through drip irrigation.

      Feeding

      Blueberries take up nitrogen best as ammonium, so use a fertilizer made for acid-loving plants or ammonium sulfate rather than nitrate fertilizers. Split it into two or three small doses from early to late spring. Too much at once burns the shallow roots. Skip fresh manure, which is salty and tends to raise pH.

      Pollination and pruning

      Northern highbush blueberries are partly self-fertile, but planting two or more varieties that bloom at the same time gives bigger berries and heavier crops through cross-pollination. They also need a winter chill of roughly 800 to 1,000 hours below 45°F, which Northwest winters easily provide.

      Fruit forms on wood that grew the previous summer, and canes are most productive at about three to six years old. Each winter after the plants are established, remove a few of the oldest, grayest canes at the base and thin out weak, twiggy growth. That keeps young, fruitful canes coming.

      Pests and diseases

      Birds are the biggest loss, so net bushes as berries begin to color. Spotted wing drosophila, a small fruit fly that arrived in the Northwest around 2009, lays eggs in ripening berries, especially later in summer. Picking every few days and refrigerating berries promptly limits the damage. Mummy berry, a fungus that turns berries into shriveled gray mummies, overwinters in fallen fruit; rake up mummies and refresh the mulch in late winter.

      Harvest and storage

      Blueberries don't get sweeter after picking. Wait several days after they turn fully blue; they keep sweetening on the bush and drop easily into your hand when ripe. Refrigerate them unwashed, and rinse just before eating. They also freeze well spread on a tray before bagging.

      Good neighbors

      Good neighbors

      • Huckleberries and other acid lovers. Same acidic, moist soil.
      • Another blueberry variety. Cross-pollination gives bigger crops.

      Pests and problems

      What most often goes wrong with blueberries in Northwest gardens, how to spot it and what to do.

      Birds

      Signs: Berries vanish as they turn blue.

      What to do: Net the bushes as the fruit colors.

      Spotted wing drosophila

      Signs: Soft, leaking berries with tiny larvae inside.

      What to do: Pick often, pick everything ripe and chill the harvest.

      Mummy berry

      Signs: Berries shrivel into gray-tan mummies.

      What to do: Rake up and bin mummies, and mulch fresh in early spring.

      Common questions about growing blueberries

      When should I plant blueberries in Oregon, Washington and Idaho?

      Plant in the garden: Mar 15 – May 3 in Portland and Seattle, Apr 15 – Jun 3 in Spokane, Apr 1 – May 20 in Boise and May 12 – Jun 30 in Bend. Pick your town at the top of the page for your own dates.

      Why are my blueberry leaves turning yellow?

      Yellow leaves with green veins usually mean the soil pH is too high, so the plant can't take up iron. Test the soil; if it's above about 5.5, work in elemental sulfur and switch to an acid-plant fertilizer. Overall pale leaves can also mean the plant simply needs nitrogen.

      Do I need two blueberry bushes?

      One bush will fruit, but two or more varieties that bloom at the same time give bigger berries and heavier crops through cross-pollination. Choosing early, mid and late varieties also spreads the harvest over the summer.

      Do coffee grounds make soil acidic enough for blueberries?

      Not reliably. Brewed grounds are close to neutral and change soil pH very little. They're fine as mulch or compost, but use elemental sulfur to actually lower pH, and test to confirm.

      How long until blueberries produce?

      Expect small crops in the second or third year and full crops by about year five. Picking off the flowers in the first year helps the plant build roots, and a healthy bush can produce for decades.
