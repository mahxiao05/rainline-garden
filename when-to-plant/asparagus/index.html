    When to plant asparagus

    Plant in the garden: Portland and Seattle Mar 15 – Apr 26; Spokane Apr 15 – May 27; Boise Apr 1 – May 13; Bend May 12 – Jun 23.

    Your dates in

      Asparagus is a long-lived perennial grown for its spring spears, the young shoots that push up from an underground crown. Spears left unpicked open into tall, ferny plants that feed the crown for next year. Washington is one of the country's main asparagus states, with most of it grown in the Columbia Basin and Yakima Valley.

      How to grow asparagus

      1. Pick a sunny spot with soil that drains well, and mix in an inch or two of compost.
      2. Plant asparagus crowns on your town's dates, 12 to 18 inches apart. In a trench 6 to 8 inches deep, covered gradually as the spears grow.
      3. Fill the trench in gradually as the shoots grow. Keep the bed weeded, and cut the ferns down only after they turn brown in fall.
      4. Harvest: Snap spears at soil level when they're 6 to 8 inches tall. Stop picking when new spears are pencil-thin, and let the ferns grow.

      Growing asparagus in your part of the Northwest

      The same plant behaves differently on the wet west side, the foggy coast and the dry, colder east side.

      West-side valleys

      Willamette Valley, Puget Sound, southern Oregon

      Asparagus crowns rot in wet, heavy soil; plant in raised beds. Slugs damage spears.

      East of the Cascades

      Columbia Basin, Yakima, Boise, Spokane, north Idaho

      The Columbia Basin grows asparagus commercially. Asparagus beetles are the main pest.

      High desert and mountain towns

      Central Oregon, high eastern Oregon valleys, McCall, Salmon

      Asparagus is very cold hardy. Late frosts can blacken early spears, but new ones keep coming.

      All-male hybrid

      Thick, tender spears and strong resistance to fusarium, rust and crown rot. It tolerates heavier soils, a plus in many west-side yards.

      How much to grow

      How many to plant

      For a family of four, 20 to 30 crowns. Plant once; a bed produces for 15 to 20 years.

      What to expect

      About ½ pound of spears per crown each spring once established, from the third year.

      Storing the harvest

      Stand spears upright in an inch of water in the fridge; they keep about a week. Blanch and freeze extras.

      Growing notes

      What makes the difference in Northwest gardens, in plain language.

      Start with the right bed

      An asparagus bed stays put for a couple of decades, so preparation pays off. Choose full sun and soil that drains well, since crowns rot in waterlogged ground. Asparagus likes a near-neutral pH of about 6.5 to 7.5; many west-side soils are more acidic and benefit from lime worked in before planting. Clear out perennial weeds like quackgrass and bindweed completely first; they're almost impossible to remove once crowns are in.

      All-male varieties like Jersey Knight and Millennium produce more spears because they don't spend energy on seeds.

      Why the ferns matter

      Every spear you pick is food the crown stored the year before. After the harvest window ends, the spears grow into tall ferns that make food through summer and send it down to the roots for next spring. That's why you don't pick the first year, pick only a couple of weeks the second year, and why you leave ferns standing until they turn brown in fall.

      Feed with compost or a balanced fertilizer after harvest ends, when the ferns are building next year's crop.

      Beetles, east and west

      Asparagus beetles, small blue-black and orange insects, chew spears and lay dark eggs on them, and their gray larvae strip the ferns. Hand-pick adults in cool mornings, rub off eggs and cut brown ferns in fall to remove their winter cover.

      Asparagus does especially well in eastern Washington and Oregon, where hot summers grow strong ferns and cold winters give a full dormancy. West-side beds produce well too, just a little later, and good drainage matters more there.

      Good neighbors

      Good neighbors

      • Parsley and basil. Fill the bed's edges in summer without disturbing the deep crowns.

      Keep apart

      • Annual vegetables in the same bed. Digging and replanting around the crowns damages them; give asparagus a permanent bed.

      Pests and problems

      What most often goes wrong with asparagus in Northwest gardens, how to spot it and what to do.

      Asparagus beetles

      Signs: Black-and-cream or orange beetles chewing spears, and black eggs on the tips.

      What to do: Hand-pick in the morning and cut spears promptly.

      Weeds

      Signs: Grass and weeds choke the bed by midsummer.

      What to do: Mulch thickly and weed in early spring before spears come up.

      Thin, weak spears

      Signs: Spears shrink to pencil size.

      What to do: Stop picking for the year, let the ferns grow and feed the bed with compost.

      Common questions about growing asparagus

      When should I plant asparagus in Oregon, Washington and Idaho?

      Plant in the garden: Mar 15 – Apr 26 in Portland and Seattle, Apr 15 – May 27 in Spokane, Apr 1 – May 13 in Boise and May 12 – Jun 23 in Bend. Pick your town at the top of the page for your own dates.

      Why are my asparagus spears so thin?

      Thin spears usually mean a young bed, a crown that was over-picked, or weak ferns the previous summer. Stop picking when spears drop to pencil-thickness, feed after harvest and let the ferns grow freely to rebuild the crowns.

      When should I cut back asparagus ferns?

      Wait until they turn yellow or brown in fall, after the plant has moved its stored food down to the crowns. Cutting them green weakens next year's harvest. Removing old ferns also takes away a winter hiding place for asparagus beetles.

      Can I harvest asparagus the first year?

      It's best not to. First-year crowns need every spear to grow ferns and build roots. Pick lightly for about two weeks in year two, then harvest fully for six to eight weeks from year three on.
