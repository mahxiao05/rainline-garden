    How to grow radicchio

    1. Start seeds indoors on your city's dates in trays or small pots under a grow light or in your sunniest window. Or skip this step and buy young plants (starts) at a garden center.
    2. Harden off the young plants: about a week before planting, set them outside for a few hours a day, a little longer each day.
    3. Plant them in the garden on your city's dates, 8 to 12 inches apart. If you plant seeds instead, sow them ¼ inch deep.
    4. Water deeply once or twice a week rather than a little every day, and mulch once the soil is warm.
    5. Plant again on your city's dates for a fall harvest.
    6. Harvest: Cut heads at the base when they feel firm, usually in fall. Leaves stay green and loose in summer heat; the red color and tight heads come with cool nights.

    Bolting from spring sowings, and slugs hiding in the heads in fall. Sow in midsummer for fall heads, and pick by variety: modern hybrids head up more reliably.

    Growing notes

    What makes the difference in Northwest gardens, in plain language.

    Why timing matters

    Radicchio is a chicory, grown for its bitter, red-and-white heads. Like other chicories it responds to day length and temperature: plants sown in spring often send up a flower stalk in summer instead of forming a head.

    That's why most Northwest growers sow in June or July, alone or in trays to transplant. Plants grow leaves through summer and form tight heads as nights cool in fall. The cold also turns the leaves a deeper red and takes the edge off the bitterness.

    Planting and growing

    Sow seeds ¼ inch deep, or start them in trays and transplant at about four weeks old, 8 to 12 inches apart. Keep the soil evenly moist through summer heat so plants don't stall.

    Don't worry if summer leaves look green and loose. The outer leaves protect the head, which forms in the center later. Modern hybrids like Indigo head up more reliably than older open-pollinated types.

    Harvest and storage

    Cut heads at the base when they feel firm, from September through late fall. West of the Cascades, radicchio handles light frost and some varieties hold in the garden into early winter.

    Peel off and compost the tattered outer leaves; the head inside is clean. Heads keep for weeks in the fridge. If a plant never forms a head, cut it back to an inch or two in early fall and it may regrow a small one.

    Good neighbors and crop rotation

    Good neighbors

    • Lettuce. Similar needs; lettuce is picked in summer while radicchio grows.
    • Leeks. Share the bed into late fall.

    Daisy family: Lettuce and radicchio have few soil-borne problems and fit anywhere in a rotation. If you've had lettuce drop, a white mold that collapses whole plants, keep them out of that bed for at least three years.

    Common questions about growing radicchio

    Why didn't my radicchio form a head?

    Usually it was sown at the wrong time. Spring sowings often bolt, and some older varieties are unreliable. Sow a modern hybrid in midsummer and give it steady water.

    Why is my radicchio green instead of red?

    The red color develops in cool weather. Leaves stay green through summer, and heads color up as nights cool in fall.

    How do I make radicchio less bitter?

    Harvest after a few cool nights, soak cut leaves in cold water for half an hour, or grill or roast them. Cooking mellows the bitterness.
