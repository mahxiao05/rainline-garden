    How to grow fig trees

    1. Pick a sunny spot with soil that drains well, and mix in an inch or two of compost.
    2. Plant fig trees on your city's dates, 10 to 15 feet apart. Same depth as in the pot, or a little deeper.
    3. Water deeply through the first summer. Mulch the base heavily in fall, and wrap young trees in frost cloth when temperatures drop toward the teens.
    4. Harvest: Figs don't ripen after picking. Wait until they droop on the stem, feel soft and the skin starts to crack slightly.

    Varieties that do well here

    • Desert King (reliable breba crop)
    • Lattarula
    • Brown Turkey
    • Chicago Hardy

    Watch out for

    Hard freezes kill young trees back to the ground, and unripe figs from late summer won't finish in a cool fall. Pick early-ripening varieties and protect young trees in cold snaps.

    Popular fig trees varieties

    Widely sold picks that do well somewhere in Oregon or Washington. The tag says which side of the mountains suits each one best.

    Desert King

    Green fig, breba crop · Brebas ripen late July to August

    Bears a large, sweet breba crop on last year's wood, so it ripens fruit even in cool Northwest summers. Green skin and strawberry-red flesh; one of the most reliable figs west of the Cascades.

    Coast and west side

    Lattarula

    Italian honey fig · Ripens August to September

    Light green figs with amber, honey-sweet flesh. It is productive in cool summers and is one of the most widely grown figs in west-side gardens.

    West side

    Brown Turkey

    Brown fig, widely sold · Ripens August to September

    The most common fig in garden centers, with medium brown-purple fruit and mild, sweet pink flesh. Fairly cold-hardy, it often regrows and fruits even after freezing back.

    Both sides

    Chicago Hardy

    Cold-hardy brown fig · Ripens August to September

    One of the hardiest figs, it can die to the ground in a hard freeze and still fruit on new growth the same summer. Good for colder east-side or higher-elevation gardens.

    Both sides

    Olympian

    Cold-hardy purple fig · Ripens late August to September

    A large, purple-brown, sweet fig found growing in Olympia, Washington. It is known for good cold hardiness and for ripening a main crop in cool maritime summers.

    West side

    Violette de Bordeaux

    Compact dark purple fig · Ripens August to September

    Small, nearly black figs with rich, berry-like red flesh. The plant stays compact, making it a good fig for large pots that can be moved to shelter in cold snaps.

    Growing notes

    What makes the difference in Northwest gardens, in plain language.

    Two crops a year, and why it matters here

    Many figs make two crops. The breba crop forms on last year's wood and ripens in early to mid summer. The main crop forms on this year's new growth and ripens in late summer and fall. In cool west-side summers, the main crop often runs out of warmth before it finishes, so varieties that set a good breba crop, like Desert King, are the most dependable choice.

    Lattarula, Brown Turkey and Chicago Hardy can ripen a main crop in warm spots or in warmer years. Common figs like these set fruit without pollination, so one tree is plenty.

    Planting and winter protection

    Plant figs where they'll get the most heat: against a south or west wall, a fence or pavement that holds warmth. They aren't fussy about soil as long as it drains. Water deeply through the first summer, then occasionally in dry spells.

    Young trees are the most vulnerable to hard freezes. Mulch the base well in fall and wrap the tree in frost cloth when temperatures head toward the teens. Even if the top dies, figs usually regrow from the roots, though you'll lose a year or two of fruit. In cold eastern areas, grow figs in large pots and move them into an unheated garage for winter.

    Pruning and harvest

    Figs need little pruning. Remove dead and crossing wood in late winter once the worst cold is past. Avoid cutting back every branch, since the breba crop grows on last year's wood and heavy pruning removes it.

    Pick figs when they droop on the stem, feel soft and the skin starts to crack slightly. An unripe fig will not ripen on the counter. Birds and wasps find ripe figs quickly, so check daily during harvest.

    What you'll need

    • Potted fig tree
    • Frost cloth
    • Large plant container

    Common questions about growing fig trees

    Can figs grow in Seattle or Portland?

    Yes. Figs do well west of the Cascades when planted in a warm, sunny spot. Choose a variety with a reliable early crop like Desert King, and protect young trees in hard freezes.

    Why do my figs fall off before they ripen?

    Small green figs that form in late summer are main-crop figs that run out of heat in a cool fall. They drop or stay hard over winter. This is normal west of the Cascades; the breba crop the next summer is the one to count on.

    Do figs need a pollinator?

    No. The common figs sold for home gardens set fruit without pollination, so a single tree will bear on its own.
