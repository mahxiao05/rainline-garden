    How to grow raspberries

    1. Pick a sunny spot with soil that drains well, and mix in an inch or two of compost.
    2. Plant raspberry canes on your city's dates, 2 to 3 feet apart in rows. Roots covered by 2 to 3 inches of soil.
    3. Set up a simple trellis with posts and wire. After harvest, cut spent canes to the ground.
    4. Harvest: Pick when berries slip easily off the core. Check every two or three days in season.

    Varieties that do well here

    • Meeker (a Northwest favorite)
    • Tulameen
    • Heritage (fall-bearing)
    • Cascade Delight

    Watch out for

    Root rot in soggy soil. Plant in a raised row or a bed that drains well.

    Popular raspberries varieties

    Widely sold picks that do well somewhere in Oregon or Washington. The tag says which side of the mountains suits each one best.

    Meeker

    Summer-bearing, red · Ripens early July

    A Northwest favorite with firm, dark red, richly flavored berries that are excellent for jam and freezing. Productive, long-lived canes make it a dependable backbone for a home berry patch.

    Tulameen

    Summer-bearing, red · Ripens July

    Very large, glossy, sweet berries over a long picking season. It is less winter hardy than some, so it shines in the milder winters west of the Cascades.

    Cascade Delight

    Summer-bearing, red · Ripens July

    Bred in Washington with resistance to root rot, the main raspberry killer in wet Northwest soils. Large, flavorful berries make it a smart choice for heavier ground.

    Willamette

    Summer-bearing, early · Ripens late June

    An old Northwest standard with large, firm, dark red berries that ripen early. Its tart-sweet flavor holds up well in jams, pies and the freezer.

    Heritage

    Fall-bearing, red · Late August to frost

    The classic fall raspberry: very hardy, upright canes that fruit on new growth each year. Mow everything to the ground in late winter for one easy fall crop.

    How it works

    The plant biology behind the advice above, in plain language.

    How raspberry canes work

    Raspberry roots live for many years, but each cane lives only two. In its first year a cane (a primocane) grows leaves; in its second year (as a floricane) it fruits and then dies. Summer-bearing varieties like Meeker and Tulameen fruit only on second-year canes. Fall-bearing varieties like Heritage also fruit at the tips of first-year canes in late summer and fall.

    That's the key to pruning. For summer-bearers, cut the canes that just fruited to the ground after harvest, and thin the new canes to the sturdiest ones, about 6 inches apart. For fall-bearers, the simplest method is to cut every cane to the ground in late winter for one large crop in late summer.

    Drainage, and root rot

    Phytophthora root rot is the most common reason raspberries die in the Northwest. This water mold thrives in soil that stays saturated, which describes much of the region in winter. Infected plants send up weak new canes that wilt and die, and the roots turn reddish-brown inside.

    Plant in a raised row or bed at least 10 to 12 inches high, and never in a low spot where water collects. Variety matters too: Cascade Bounty and Cascade Delight are very resistant, while Meeker is moderately so. Avoid beds where tomatoes or potatoes grew recently, since they share Verticillium wilt.

    Soil, water and feeding

    Raspberries prefer a slightly acidic soil, with a pH of about 5.6 to 6.2, rich in organic matter. Give them an inch or two of water a week while fruit is forming, through drip lines or soaker hoses, so the canes and berries stay dry. Feed in early spring, when new growth starts.

    Pests

    Spotted wing drosophila, a small fruit fly, lays eggs in ripening raspberries, and fall-bearing crops are hit hardest. Pick every two days, collect any overripe fruit, and chill berries quickly. Root weevils and cane diseases also show up, but good airflow from thinning and trellising prevents most cane problems.

    Harvest

    A ripe raspberry pulls cleanly off its core with a gentle tug. Berries don't ripen after picking and are among the most perishable fruits, so pick into shallow containers, refrigerate right away and eat within two or three days, or freeze them on a tray.

    Common questions about growing raspberries

    Why are my raspberry canes dying?

    If they're canes that fruited last summer, that's normal: each cane dies after its second year. If new canes are wilting and dying, suspect root rot from soggy soil. Move the planting to a raised bed and choose a resistant variety like Cascade Bounty or Cascade Delight.

    How do I prune summer-bearing vs. fall-bearing raspberries?

    For summer-bearers, cut canes that just fruited to the ground after harvest and thin the new canes. For fall-bearers, mow or cut all canes to the ground in late winter for a single late-summer crop.

    How long do raspberry plants last?

    A well-drained planting can produce for ten years or more, since the roots keep sending up new canes. Plantings in wet soil often decline within a few years because of root rot.
